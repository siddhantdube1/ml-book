# Support vector machines

*The best boundary, not just a boundary — the line with the widest margin of
safety, the handful of points that hold it in place, and the kernel trick
that lets a straight cut curve into any shape you like.*

Picture a straight road you have to paint between two rows of parked cars.
Plenty of lines would keep you clear of both rows — hug the left, hug the
right, cut a slight diagonal — and a careless painter might choose any of
them. But there is exactly one line that runs down the true middle, leaving
the most clearance on either side, and it is obviously the one you would
want to drive: the line with the most room to be wrong before you clip a
mirror.

Chapter 7 was the careless painter. Logistic regression drew *a* boundary
that separated the classes and stopped, never asking whether some other
boundary sat more safely between them. The support vector machine asks
precisely that. Of all the lines that separate the two classes, it finds the
one with the widest *margin* — the largest gap to the nearest point on
either side — on the wager that the most cautious boundary is the one most
likely to survive contact with data it has never seen.

That wager leads somewhere remarkable. We will find that only a handful of
points — the ones pressed right up against the margin — decide the boundary
at all; that the search for the widest margin is a clean convex problem with
a single best answer; and that with one of the most elegant manoeuvres in
the subject, the *kernel trick*, the very same machinery that draws a
straight line can be made to draw a circle, a pair of arcs, or almost any
curve you like — without ever leaving the equation for a line.

## 1. The best line

Every classifier so far has been content to find *a* boundary. The
perceptron stopped at the first line that separated the data; logistic
regression settled on the line that maximised the likelihood. Neither asked
whether the boundary sat *well* — whether it left room on both sides, or
skimmed perilously close to the nearest points.

But intuitively, room matters. A boundary that passes a hair's breadth from a
training point is a boundary one noisy measurement away from getting that
point wrong; a boundary that keeps a wide berth from every point has slack to
spare. If you believe future data will look roughly like the training data,
the safest bet is the boundary that is *as far as possible* from the closest
points of either class. That distance has a name — the **margin** — and
maximising it is the entire idea of the support vector machine.

This one principle, "widest margin", will carry us through soft margins,
the hinge loss, and ultimately the kernel trick. Let us make it precise.

## 2. Margin and the widest street

Take a linear boundary, written as $w \cdot x + b = 0$: the set of points
where the weighted sum is zero, with everything on one side classified
positive and the other negative, exactly as in Chapter 7. The vector $w$ is
*perpendicular* to this boundary, and the signed distance from any point $x$
to it is $(w\cdot x + b)/\lVert w\rVert$.

The **margin** is the distance from the boundary to the *nearest* training
point. Think of it as the half-width of the widest "street" you can paint
down the middle of the data without any point falling inside: the boundary is
the centre line, and the two kerbs are the parallel lines that just touch the
closest points of each class. Tilt or shift the centre line and the street
you can fit narrows; the SVM chooses the orientation and position that make
the street as wide as possible.

<MaxMargin />

Drag the candidate to any orientation other than the SVM's and its best
possible street is narrower — the maximum-margin boundary really is the
widest one. Notice, too, how few points actually touch the kerbs.

## 3. Support vectors

Look again at which points sit against the margin. On the maximum-margin
boundary, only a handful do — and those few are the only points that matter.
Pick up any of the others and slide it around: as long as it stays off the
street, the boundary does not move an inch. The optimum is pinned in place
entirely by the points pressed against its kerbs.

These critical points are the **support vectors**, and the model takes its
name from them. It is a striking kind of sparsity: a dataset of thousands of
points may be summarised, for the purpose of drawing the boundary, by a mere
dozen support vectors — the ones living dangerously on the margin. Everything
comfortably classified is irrelevant to the fit.

This has two consequences worth holding onto. First, the SVM is robust to the
bulk of the data — outliers far on the correct side change nothing. Second,
and we will cash this in later, if only the support vectors matter, then the
whole model can be written in terms of them — which is exactly the opening
the kernel trick will walk through.

## 4. The optimisation problem

Let us turn "widest margin" into an equation. There is a redundancy to fix
first: we can scale $w$ and $b$ by any positive constant without moving the
boundary $w\cdot x + b = 0$. We use that freedom to *fix the scale* so that
the closest points satisfy $\lvert w\cdot x + b\rvert = 1$. With that
convention, every point obeys

$$y_i\,(w\cdot x_i + b) \ge 1,$$

where $y_i = \pm 1$ is the label, and the support vectors meet it with
equality. The distance from the boundary to those closest points is then
exactly $1/\lVert w\rVert$, so the margin is $1/\lVert w\rVert$ and the full
width of the street is $2/\lVert w\rVert$.

Maximising the margin therefore means *minimising* $\lVert w\rVert$. Squaring
for convenience, the SVM solves

$$\min_{w,\,b}\ \tfrac12 \lVert w\rVert^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w\cdot x_i + b)\ge 1 \ \text{for all } i.$$

This is a **convex quadratic program**: a bowl-shaped objective under linear
constraints, with a single global optimum and no local minima to trap us —
the same comforting geometry as the convex losses of Chapters 6–7. And notice
the objective is exactly the L2 penalty of Chapter 9: minimising $\lVert
w\rVert^2$ *is* regularisation, here handed a crisp geometric meaning — small
weights mean a wide margin.

## 5. Soft margins: when the classes overlap

The clean story assumes the classes can be separated at all. Real data
rarely obliges: clouds overlap, labels are noisy, and no straight line gets
everything right. A hard margin would have no solution.

The fix is to allow violations, but charge for them. We give each point a
*slack* $\xi_i \ge 0$ measuring how far it intrudes past its margin — zero
for points safely outside, positive for points inside the street or on the
wrong side — and add the total slack to the objective with a price $C$:

$$\min_{w,\,b,\,\xi}\ \tfrac12 \lVert w\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w\cdot x_i + b)\ge 1 - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0.$$

The single knob $C$ sets the exchange rate between a wide margin and few
violations. A **large** $C$ makes every violation expensive, forcing a
narrow, hard margin that contorts to classify nearly every training point —
low bias, high variance. A **small** $C$ shrugs off violations to keep the
margin wide and smooth, accepting some misclassifications — high bias, low
variance. It is the bias–variance trade-off of Chapters 9–11 in a new guise.

<SoftMargin />

Sweep $C$ from small to large and watch the street pinch in as the boundary
starts chasing individual points, the support-vector count shifting with it.
The right $C$ is the one that generalises best — found, as ever, by
cross-validation.

## 6. The hinge loss: an SVM is a linear model with a different loss

The soft-margin problem looks like a constrained program, but it hides a
familiar shape. At the optimum each slack takes its smallest allowed value,
$\xi_i = \max(0,\ 1 - y_i(w\cdot x_i + b))$ — zero when the point is safely
past its margin, and growing linearly as it intrudes. That expression is the
**hinge loss**. Substituting it back turns the SVM into a plain unconstrained
minimisation:

$$\min_{w}\ \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2 + \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\!\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(w\cdot x_i + b)\big).$$

This is *exactly* the form of Chapters 6–7: a regularisation term plus an
average loss. The SVM is simply a linear model trained with the **hinge loss**
in place of logistic regression's log-loss, and $\lambda$ (inversely related
to $C$) is the L2 strength. Two linear classifiers, same machinery,
different loss.

<HingeLoss />

The difference between the two losses is the whole personality of the SVM.
Both upper-bound the 0–1 error, so minimising either drives the true error
down — but the hinge loss is *flat* once a point is past the margin. A point
sitting comfortably on the right side contributes zero loss and zero
gradient: it is ignored. Logistic regression's loss, by contrast, never quite
reaches zero, so every point keeps tugging on the boundary forever. The
hinge's flat region is precisely why only the support vectors — the points
still on or inside the margin — determine the SVM's solution.

And because this is just regularised loss minimisation, you can train a
linear SVM by the gradient descent of Chapter 6: follow the hinge loss's
(sub)gradient downhill. That is exactly the algorithm we implement in §11.

## 7. The kernel trick (1): lifting to where it separates

So far, straight boundaries. But some data is hopelessly non-linear — a class
encircled by another, say, with no line able to separate the ring from its
core. Logistic regression would fail here, and so would a linear SVM.

The escape is a beautiful change of perspective. Take each 2-D point
$(x_1, x_2)$ and *lift* it into three dimensions by adding a coordinate
$z = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ — its squared distance from the origin. Points near the
centre lift only a little; points far out lift high. The inner class sinks
into the bottom of a bowl while the outer class rides up its sides, and now a
single flat **plane**, slid horizontally between them, separates the two
perfectly. A linear boundary — in the lifted space.

<KernelLift />

Project that separating plane back down to the original plane and its shadow
is a **circle**. The non-linear boundary we wanted is nothing more than a
linear boundary viewed from a higher dimension. Lift the data into a richer
space, separate it with a flat plane there, and the flatness becomes
curvature on the way back down. The only question left is the cost of the
lift — and that is where the trick becomes genius.

## 8. The kernel trick (2): computing the lift for free

Building an explicit lift is expensive, and for the most useful lifts —
which run to thousands or even infinitely many new coordinates — outright
impossible. Here the support vectors of §3 pay off. Because the SVM's
solution depends on the data *only through dot products* $x_i \cdot x_j$ (a
fact that falls out of the so-called dual form of the problem), we never need
the lifted coordinates themselves — only the dot products *between* lifted
points.

A **kernel** is a function that returns exactly that dot product, computed
directly from the original points without ever building the lift:

$$K(x_i, x_j) = \phi(x_i)\cdot\phi(x_j),$$

where $\phi$ is the lift. The two that matter most:

- the **polynomial kernel** $K(x_i,x_j) = (x_i\cdot x_j + 1)^d$, whose lift is
  all products of up to $d$ features;
- the **RBF (Gaussian) kernel** $K(x_i,x_j) = \exp(-\gamma\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert^2)$,
  whose lift is *infinite-dimensional* — and which, astonishingly, you compute
  with one subtraction, one exponential, and a single parameter $\gamma$.

Swap every dot product in the SVM for $K$ and the linear machine draws
arbitrarily curved boundaries, at the cost of evaluating $K$. That swap — the
**kernel trick** — is the move that made SVMs famous.

## 9. Choosing the kernel and its parameters

The RBF kernel's width $\gamma$ is a complexity knob, and you saw it bite in
the widget above. A **small** $\gamma$ means each point's influence reaches
far, giving a smooth, almost-linear boundary that can underfit. A **large**
$\gamma$ means influence is intensely local, so the boundary wraps tight
islands around individual points — training accuracy soars, validation
accuracy collapses, and the support-vector count balloons: textbook
overfitting. Together with $C$, that is two hyperparameters, and they are
chosen exactly as Chapter 11 prescribes — a grid search over $(C, \gamma)$
scored by cross-validation.

Three practical notes. **Standardise your features first**: the RBF kernel
measures distances, so a feature on a larger scale would silently dominate.
**Try the linear kernel before the RBF**: when there are already many
features (text, genomics), the data is often linearly separable and the
simpler model wins. And remember the kernel matrix is $n\times n$, which
brings us to the costs.

## 10. Multi-class, and where SVMs sit

The SVM is intrinsically **binary** — one boundary, two sides. For $k$
classes you fall back on Chapter 8's recipes: *one-vs-rest* (train $k$
classifiers, each one class against all others, take the most confident) or
*one-vs-one* (train a classifier for every pair and vote). Both wrap the
binary SVM without changing it.

Where does all this leave the SVM in practice? Its **strengths** are real:
superb in high dimensions, effective even when features outnumber samples
($p \gg n$, as in text classification), elegant non-linearity through
kernels, and a sparse solution resting on a few support vectors.
Its **weaknesses** are equally real: the $n\times n$ kernel matrix makes
kernel SVMs scale poorly past tens of thousands of points; they output
distances, not calibrated probabilities, natively; and they demand careful
feature scaling and hyperparameter tuning.

For two decades the kernel SVM was the strongest off-the-shelf classifier in
the world. Today gradient boosting usually wins on tabular data and neural
networks on perception, and the SVM has settled into a specialist's tool —
excellent on high-dimensional, scarce, or text data. But the ideas it
crystallised run straight through modern machine learning.

## 11. Implementing it yourself

A linear soft-margin SVM by **Pegasos** — stochastic sub-gradient descent on
the regularised hinge loss of §6. The whole optimiser is the inner loop:
shrink $w$ toward zero (the regulariser), and for any point still inside its
margin, nudge $w$ toward classifying it correctly (the hinge sub-gradient).

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

# Two separable blobs: class 0 left, class 1 right.
def make_blobs(n, seed, gap=3.0, spread=0.45):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    y = np.arange(n) % 2
    cx = np.where(y == 0, -gap / 2, gap / 2)
    cy = np.where(y == 0, 0.35, -0.35)
    X = np.stack([rng.normal(cx, spread), rng.normal(cy, spread)], axis=1)
    return X, y

def pegasos(X, y, C, epochs, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = X.shape
    s = 2 * y - 1                       # {0,1} -> {-1,+1}
    lam = 1.0 / (C * n)                 # regularisation strength
    w = np.zeros(d); b = 0.0; t = 0
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for i in rng.permutation(n):
            t += 1
            eta = 1.0 / (lam * t)       # decaying step size
            if s[i] * (w @ X[i] + b) < 1:           # inside the margin
                w = (1 - eta * lam) * w + eta * s[i] * X[i]
                b += eta * s[i]
            else:                                   # safely outside
                w = (1 - eta * lam) * w
    return w, b

X, y = make_blobs(80, 7)
w, b = pegasos(X, y, C=1.0, epochs=300)

pred = (X @ w + b >= 0).astype(int)
margin = 1.0 / np.linalg.norm(w)
s = 2 * y - 1
n_sv = int(np.sum(s * (X @ w + b) <= 1 + 1e-6))   # points on/inside the margin
print(f"training accuracy: {(pred == y).mean():.3f}")
print(f"margin 1/||w||:    {margin:.3f}")
print(f"support vectors:   {n_sv}")
`}
/>

It separates the blobs perfectly and recovers a wide margin from a couple of
support vectors. The only thing between this and a production linear SVM is
better step-size bookkeeping; the only thing between it and a *kernel* SVM is
the dual formulation of §8, which replaces $w$ with a weighted sum over
support vectors and every dot product with $K$.

## 12. Problems

### Problem 1 — Margins and support vectors

A conceptual question. (a) Two different lines both separate the training
data perfectly. Why does the SVM prefer the one with the larger margin —
what does margin have to do with generalisation? (b) A point sits far from
the boundary, correctly classified. Delete it and retrain: does the boundary
move? What if instead you delete a support vector? (c) As you *decrease* $C$,
what happens to the width of the margin and to the number of support
vectors, and why?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** The margin is the boundary's distance to the nearest training point —
its buffer against noise. A wide margin means every point sits well clear of
the boundary, so a small perturbation (a noisy feature, an unseen point near
a training one) is unlikely to land on the wrong side. A boundary that
skims close to points has no such slack. Formally, a larger margin
corresponds to a smaller $\lVert w\rVert$ — less model complexity, the L2
regularisation of Chapter 9 — and so to a tighter bound on the gap between
training and test error.

**(b)** Deleting a non-support point changes *nothing*: the boundary is
determined entirely by the support vectors on the margin, and a point safely
off the street contributes zero hinge loss and zero gradient. Deleting a
*support vector* generally *does* move the boundary — it was one of the few
points actively holding it in place. This is the sparsity that defines the
model.

**(c)** Decreasing $C$ makes violations cheaper, so the optimiser happily
widens the margin even though more points end up inside it. The margin grows
and the number of support vectors *increases* (more points lie on or within
the wider street). Large $C$ does the reverse: a narrow, hard margin with few
support vectors. Small $C$ = more regularisation = higher bias, lower
variance.

</details>

### Problem 5 — The kernel trick from scratch

The capstone. Take the concentric rings, confirm a linear SVM fails, then

def make_rings(n, seed, noise=0.13):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

The support vector machine is the most geometric model in the book. Every
piece of it is a picture: the widest street between the classes, the handful
of support vectors holding the boundary in place, the lift into a higher
dimension where the tangled becomes separable and the plane's shadow falls
back as a curve. And underneath the geometry sits the familiar machinery — a
regularised loss, minimised by gradient descent, tuned by cross-validation —
so the SVM is at once the most visual classical model and a close cousin of
the linear models we began with.
