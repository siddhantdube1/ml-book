# Backpropagation and training

*How a network learns: the chain rule turned into an algorithm that finds every
weight's gradient at once — and the craft of actually training the thing.*

A trained network is a marvel of cooperation: a million numbers, spread across a
dozen layers, that together turn a photograph into the word "cat". An untrained
one is a million numbers chosen at random, and it turns the same photograph into
noise. Everything that separates the two — all of the intelligence — lives in the
gap, and getting from one to the other means answering a question that sounds
impossible. The network makes a mistake; the error arrives as a single number at
the very end. Which of the million weights, buried layers deep, was to blame, and
by how much should each one change?

You could imagine finding out by brute force: nudge one weight a hair, run the
whole network again, and see whether the error went up or down. Do that for every
weight and you would know the gradient — the direction to step each one — exactly.
You would also need a million forward passes for a single step of learning, and a
billion-weight network would never finish its first. Brute force is a non-starter
by a factor of millions, and for a long time the lack of anything better is what
kept neural networks a curiosity.

The thing that is better is **backpropagation**, and its secret is almost
deflating: it is just the chain rule from first-year calculus, applied with enough
care to share its work. In a single backward sweep through the network — one extra
pass, not a million — it computes the gradient of the loss with respect to *every*
weight at once. That one idea is what turned neural networks from a curiosity into
the most powerful technology of the age, and it is, at bottom, a piece of
bookkeeping. This chapter derives it, watches it run, and then turns to the craft
that surrounds it — learning rates, optimisers, bumpy landscapes, and the
ever-present danger of a network that memorises instead of learns.

## 1. The credit-assignment problem

Frame the difficulty precisely, because backprop is the answer to it. Training is
gradient descent (Chapter 6): to improve the network, we need $\partial L /
\partial w$ for every weight $w$ — how the loss would change if we nudged that one
weight. With those gradients in hand, we step each weight a little in the
direction that lowers the loss, and repeat.

The trouble is computing them. A weight in the first layer affects the loss only
through a long chain — it changes a hidden unit, which changes the next layer,
which changes the next, all the way to the output. Its influence is tangled
together with every other weight's. The brute-force approach — perturb it, re-run
the network, measure the change — gives one gradient per forward pass, so a
network with a million weights needs a million forward passes for a *single*
gradient-descent step. Modern networks have billions. This is the
**credit-assignment problem**: given one number of error at the end, fairly
distribute the blame across millions of weights at the start. We need all the
gradients from essentially one pass, not a million.

## 2. The chain rule, the only idea you need

The whole solution rests on a fact you already know. A neural network is a deep
*composition* of simple functions — layer after layer, each feeding the next — and
calculus tells us exactly how to differentiate a composition. If $L$ depends on
$x$ through an intermediate $g$, then

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial g} \cdot \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}.$$

The gradient of a composition is the *product* of the local gradients along the
chain. To find how a weight deep in the network affects the loss, you multiply
together the local derivatives of every step between them. That is all
backpropagation is — the chain rule, applied to the network's composition. The
cleverness is purely organisational: many of those chains share the same
beginnings, so if you compute them in the right order — from the output backward —
each shared piece is computed once and reused. Brute force redoes the shared work
a million times; backprop does it once.

## 3. The forward pass remembers

Those local derivatives depend on the values flowing through the network — a
unit's gradient involves its own activation and weighted sum. So backprop begins
by running the **forward pass** (Chapter 21) and *storing* every intermediate: each
layer's weighted sum $z$ and activation $a$. Think of the network as a
**computational graph**, a chain of little operations. The forward pass sweeps
left to right and fills in every node's *value*; the backward pass will sweep right
to left and fill in every node's *gradient*. Run forward, remember everything,
then run backward. With that, we can watch the algorithm itself.

## 4. Backpropagation: the backward pass

Here is the algorithm, made concrete on a tiny network and one example.

<Backprop />

Step through it. The forward pass fills each node with its value and produces a
loss. Then the error is pushed *backward*: it starts at the output as the simple
difference between prediction and target, and each layer, working right to left,
takes the gradient on its output and turns it into two things — the gradient on
its *weights* (what we are after), and the gradient on its *input*, which it
passes to the layer before. The error flows back along the very same wires the
signal flowed forward through, and by the time the sweep reaches the first layer,
every weight in the network has its gradient. One forward pass and one backward
pass — not a million.

## 5. The shape of it: forward signal, backward error

The mechanics distil into three facts worth committing to memory, because they
*are* backpropagation. First, at the output, the error is just **prediction minus
target**, $\delta = \hat y - y$ — clean, for the sigmoid-with-cross-entropy and
softmax losses we use (the same tidy form as logistic regression in Chapter 7).
Second, to send the error back through a layer, **multiply by the weights and by
the activation's derivative**: $\delta^{(l-1)} = \big(W^{(l)\top}\delta^{(l)}\big)
\odot \phi'(z^{(l-1)})$ — the transposed weights route the blame to the inputs, and
$\phi'$ accounts for the bend each unit applied. Third, each weight's gradient is
its **backward error times its forward signal**:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} = \delta_i\, a_j.$$

That last equation is the heart of it: at every weight, the error coming back from
the output meets the activation that went forward into it, and their product is
the gradient. Forward signal, backward error, multiplied — everywhere, at once.

## 6. From gradient to step: batch, mini-batch, SGD

Backprop gives the gradient for *one* example; training combines them. **Batch**
gradient descent averages the gradient over the entire dataset before each step —
smooth and accurate, but slow, and it must see all the data to move once.
**Stochastic** gradient descent (SGD) goes to the other extreme: one example per
step, so the gradient is a noisy estimate, but steps are cheap and frequent, and
the noise can even help jiggle the optimiser out of bad spots. **Mini-batch**
descent — a few dozen examples per step — is the universal compromise everyone
actually uses: enough examples to estimate the gradient decently, few enough to
step often. One full pass through the data is an **epoch**, and training runs for
many.

## 7. The learning rate, again — and why it is everything

Backprop tells you which *direction* to step each weight; the **learning rate**
tells you how *far*, and it is the single most consequential knob in training a
network — the same $\eta$ from Chapter 6, now steering millions of parameters at
once.

<LearningRateNN />

Drag it through its three regimes. Too small, and the loss inches down so slowly
that training never arrives — the boundary barely forms. Too large, and each step
overshoots the valley it was aiming for; the loss spikes and thrashes, the
boundary lurching wildly, and in the worst case it diverges to infinity. In the
band between, the loss glides smoothly down and the moons separate. Finding that
band — by trial, by schedules that shrink the rate over time, or by optimisers
that adapt it automatically (§9) — is most of the practical art of training.

## 8. The landscape is not a bowl

There is a deeper reason training is hard, and it is worth seeing directly. The
losses of Parts II and III were **convex** — single smooth bowls, where gradient
descent is guaranteed to reach the one global minimum. A neural network's loss is
nothing like that.

<LossLandscape />

This is a real slice of a tiny network's loss surface over two of its weights, and
it is **non-convex**: bumpy, with more than one valley separated by ridges.
Gradient descent has no map; it simply rolls downhill from wherever it starts, so
two different starting points settle into two different minima, and neither is
guaranteed to be the best one possible. By the theory of Chapter 6, this should be
a disaster. The astonishing empirical fact — and a large part of why deep learning
works at all — is that it is not. In the very high-dimensional weight spaces of
real networks, the bad, sharp minima turn out to be rare, good-enough valleys are
almost everywhere, and most paths downhill reach a solution that generalises
perfectly well. Non-convexity is real; in practice, it rarely bites.

## 9. Better steps: momentum and Adam

Plain gradient descent is inefficient on these landscapes — it crawls across flat
plateaus and zig-zags down narrow ravines. Two refinements fix it, and they are
what make large networks trainable in reasonable time.

<OptimizerRace />

**Momentum** (Chapter 6) gives the optimiser a velocity: it accumulates a running
average of past gradients, so it rolls *through* small bumps and *accelerates*
down consistent slopes instead of restarting its step each time. **Adam** goes
further and adapts the learning rate *per weight* — keeping a running estimate of
each gradient's typical size and dividing by it, so weights with small, steady
gradients take big steps and weights with large, erratic ones take small steps.
The race makes the difference plain: plain SGD inches down and stalls, momentum
rolls past it, and Adam reaches a low loss in a fraction of the epochs. It is the
default optimiser of modern deep learning for exactly this reason.

## 10. Why training is hard, and how we cope

Two failure modes round out the practical picture, and both connect to earlier
chapters. The first is **vanishing and exploding gradients**. The backward pass
*multiplies* a derivative at every layer, so in a deep network those factors
compound: if they are mostly less than one the gradient shrinks toward zero before
it reaches the early layers (which then barely learn), and if mostly greater than
one it blows up. The flat tails of sigmoid and tanh (Chapter 21), where $\phi'
\approx 0$, are a prime culprit — which is why **ReLU**, careful weight
**initialisation**, and normalisation techniques exist, all aimed at keeping the
backward signal alive.

The second is **overfitting**, and it returns with a vengeance, because a big
network has more than enough capacity to memorise its training set outright.

<OverfitRegularise />

With no regularisation, the network drives its training loss to zero by
memorising every noisy point — while the validation loss bottoms out and climbs,
the overfitting U of Chapters 11 and 14, now on a network, and the boundary
contorts grotesquely. The cures are the ones the whole book has built: **weight
decay** (the L2 penalty of Chapter 9, shrinking the weights), **early stopping**
(halt at the validation minimum, as in Chapters 11 and 14), and the
network-native **dropout** — randomly silencing units during training so the
network cannot lean too hard on any one of them. Turn up the weight decay and the
boundary smooths, the curves pull back together, and the network generalises
again. *Complexity*: the backward pass costs about the same as the forward pass —
on the order of the number of weights per example — which is the entire reason
backprop scales to networks with billions of them.

## 11. Implementing it yourself

def sigmoid(z):
    return np.where(z >= 0, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z)), np.exp(z) / (1 + np.exp(z)))

def make_xor(n, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    c = np.array([[1.4, 1.4], [-1.4, 1.4], [-1.4, -1.4], [1.4, -1.4]])

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
W1 = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(2/2), (h, 2)); b1 = np.zeros(h)      # 2 -> 4
W2 = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(2/h), (1, h)); b2 = np.zeros(1)      # 4 -> 1

for epoch in range(2001):
    # ── forward pass (remember Z1, A1, A2) ──
    Z1 = X @ W1.T + b1;  A1 = np.tanh(Z1)
    A2 = sigmoid(A1 @ W2.T + b2).ravel()

    # ── backward pass (the chain rule, output to input) ──
    dZ2 = (A2 - y) / n                          # error at the output: y_hat - y
    gW2 = dZ2 @ A1;  gb2 = dZ2.sum()            # grad = backward error x forward signal
    dZ1 = np.outer(dZ2, W2.ravel()) * (1 - A1**2)   # push back: x W2, x tanh'(Z1)
    gW1 = dZ1.T @ X; gb1 = dZ1.sum(0)

    # ── gradient-descent step ──
    W2 -= 0.5 * gW2; b2 -= 0.5 * gb2
    W1 -= 0.5 * gW1; b1 -= 0.5 * gb1

    if epoch % 500 == 0:
        loss = -(y*np.log(A2+1e-9) + (1-y)*np.log(1-A2+1e-9)).mean()
        acc = ((A2 >= 0.5).astype(int) == y).mean()
        print(f"epoch {epoch:4d}: loss {loss:.4f}  accuracy {acc:.3f}")
`}

The backward pass is four lines, mirroring the forward pass in reverse: the output
error $\hat y - y$, pushed back through the weights and the activation's
derivative, meeting the stored forward activations to form every gradient. Run it
and the loss falls toward zero as the network learns the XOR no single neuron could
— the whole of Chapter 21 and Chapter 22, in thirty lines.

## 12. Problems

### Problem 1 — The chain rule and the landscape

A conceptual question. (a) Why is computing gradients by perturbing each weight
and re-running the network hopeless for a large network, and how does one backward
pass fix it? (b) Why is a network's loss surface non-convex, and why does gradient
descent work on it anyway? (c) How do saturating activations like sigmoid and tanh
cause vanishing gradients?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** Perturbing one weight and re-running gives that weight's gradient at the
cost of a full forward pass, so a network with $W$ weights needs $W$ forward passes
for a single gradient-descent step — millions or billions of passes per step,
hopeless. Backpropagation computes *all* $W$ gradients in one forward pass plus one
backward pass, by applying the chain rule from the output backward and reusing the
shared sub-computations. The cost goes from $O(W)$ passes to $O(1)$.

**(b)** A network composes many non-linear layers, and the loss as a function of
its weights is correspondingly bumpy — full of local minima, saddle points, and
plateaus — rather than a single convex bowl. Gradient descent only ever rolls
downhill, so it can settle in a local minimum, with no guarantee of the global
best. It works anyway because in the very high-dimensional weight spaces of real
networks, poor sharp minima are rare and good-enough valleys are abundant, so
almost any downhill path reaches a solution that generalises well.

**(c)** The backward pass multiplies by each layer's activation derivative
$\phi'(z)$. Sigmoid and tanh are flat at their extremes, where $\phi'(z) \approx
0$, so when units saturate the gradient is multiplied by near-zero factors layer
after layer and shrinks toward nothing before it reaches the early layers — which
therefore barely learn. ReLU, whose derivative is exactly 1 for positive inputs,
avoids this.

</details>
