# Naive Bayes

*A classifier built almost entirely from Bayes' rule and one brazenly-false
assumption — fast, frugal with data, and despite the lie at its core, the
spam filter that actually worked.*

A test for a rare disease is, the leaflet promises, "99% accurate." You test
positive. Most people read that number and panic — and most people are wrong,
often wildly, because the leaflet's 99% answers a different question from the
one that matters. The question you care about is *given that I tested
positive, what is the chance I am actually sick?* — and if the disease is rare
enough, the answer can be a reassuring few percent even after a positive test.
The gap between those two numbers is the single most misunderstood idea in
probability, and closing it takes exactly one formula: Bayes' rule.

Naive Bayes is that formula scaled up into a classifier. Where logistic
regression and the support vector machine drew a boundary and asked which side
a point fell on, naive Bayes does something stranger and older. For each class
it builds a little model of *what that class's data looks like* — what a spam
email tends to contain, what a healthy blood sample tends to read — and then,
faced with a new point, it asks which class was most likely to have produced
it. It reasons backwards from effect to cause, which is what Bayes' rule is
for.

The catch is in the name. To make the backwards reasoning tractable, naive
Bayes assumes every feature is independent of every other within a class — an
assumption so brazenly false that it is almost funny, and yet the classifier
it produces is fast, frugal with data, and very hard to beat as a first
attempt, especially on text. By the end of this chapter you will have built a
working spam filter out of nothing but counting and Bayes' rule, seen exactly
what the naive assumption throws away, and understood the lovely paradox at
the centre of the method: that being wrong about the probabilities can still
mean being right about the answer.

## 1. The reverend's classifier

Every classifier so far has been *discriminative*. Logistic regression, the
SVM, the trees — each one models the boundary between classes directly, asking
$P(y \mid x)$: given this input, which label? They never pause to wonder what
the inputs of each class actually look like; they only care where to cut.

Naive Bayes belongs to the other great family, the *generative* classifiers,
and the difference is a genuine change of worldview. A generative model asks,
for each class, $P(x \mid y)$: if I told you this email were spam, what words
would you expect to see? It learns a little story of how each class produces
its data. Then, handed a new point, it runs every class's story forward, sees
which one was most likely to have generated what you observed, and picks that
one. Discriminative models learn to *tell the classes apart*; generative
models learn to *imitate each class*, and classify by asking who imitates the
data best.

That backwards step — from "what does each class generate" to "which class did
this come from" — is exactly the inversion Bayes' rule performs. So before any
classifier, we need the rule itself.

## 2. Bayes' rule for classification

Bayes' rule relates the two conditional probabilities you can run in either
direction:

$$P(y \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid y)\,P(y)}{P(x)}.$$

Read it as a recipe. $P(y)$ is the **prior** — how common the class is before
you look at the features (spam is, say, 40% of mail). $P(x \mid y)$ is the
**likelihood** — how well class $y$'s story explains the data you see.
Multiply them and you get something proportional to the **posterior** $P(y
\mid x)$, the thing you actually want: the probability of the class given the
evidence. To classify, compute this for every class and take the largest.

The denominator $P(x)$ is the probability of the data under *any* class. It is
the same for every class, so when you compare classes it cancels — and the
whole decision reduces to

$$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{y}\ P(x \mid y)\,P(y).$$

Likelihood times prior, biggest wins. That is the entire generative recipe,
and it is also the resolution of the disease-test paradox: the test's accuracy
is a likelihood, but the answer you want is a posterior, and the prior — the
rarity of the disease — is exactly the term that the panicked reading forgets.
Everything now hinges on one hard piece: the likelihood $P(x \mid y)$.

## 3. The trouble with the likelihood

Here is the wall. $P(x \mid y)$ is the probability of an entire feature vector
at once — every feature, jointly. Estimating it honestly means knowing, for
each class, how probable *every combination* of feature values is. With a
handful of binary features that is already a table of $2^p$ entries; with the
ten-thousand-word vocabulary of an email it is a number with thousands of
digits. You would need more emails than there are atoms in the galaxy to fill
that table with reliable counts.

This is the curse of dimensionality wearing a new hat. Bayes' rule is exact
and gives us precisely the quantity we want, but the likelihood at its heart
is a joint distribution over all features, and joint distributions in high
dimensions are unknowable from any realistic amount of data. The rule is
useless unless we can cheapen the likelihood — drastically.

## 4. The naive assumption

Here is the cheat, and it is the whole method. *Assume that, within a class,
the features are independent of one another.* Then the joint likelihood
factors into a product of one-feature likelihoods:

$$P(x \mid y) = P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{p} P(x_j \mid y).$$

The impossible joint table collapses into $p$ little one-dimensional
distributions per class — a mean and variance for each feature, or a single
word probability. Instead of an astronomically large count, you estimate $p
\times k$ easy ones, each from the whole dataset. The classifier becomes
trivial to fit and instant to evaluate.

Is the assumption true? Almost never. The word "money" and the word "free"
co-occur in spam far more than independence would predict; pixels next to each
other are correlated; symptoms cluster. Naive Bayes cheerfully ignores all of
it and pretends every feature is its own unrelated witness. The audacity is
the point — and the rest of the chapter is about cashing in the convenience
(§5, §7), measuring what the lie costs (§6), and understanding why, against
all odds, it so often does not matter (§10).

## 5. Gaussian naive Bayes

For continuous features, the natural choice for each little one-dimensional
likelihood is a **Gaussian**. Gaussian naive Bayes models each feature, within
each class, as a bell curve — estimate its mean and variance from that class's
training points, and you are done. The likelihood of a point is the product of
its per-feature bell-curve heights; multiply by the prior, and you have the
(unnormalised) posterior for each class.

$$P(y = c \mid x) \propto P(c)\prod_{j=1}^{p} \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_j;\ \mu_{cj},\ \sigma_{cj}^2\big).$$

Because everything is a product of exponentials, it is cleaner to work in
logs — sum the log-priors and log-Gaussians — but the idea is exactly the
picture below.

<ClassConditional />

Each class is just one Gaussian per feature — the bell curves drawn along the
top and the right margins. Multiply them, weight by the prior, normalise, and
the smooth posterior field appears, its half-way line the decision boundary.
Slide the prior and the boundary slides too: make the orange class rare and it
must clear a higher bar of evidence before naive Bayes is willing to call a
point orange. That is "$\times\,P(y)$" made visible — the same prior that
rescued us from the disease-test paradox.

## 6. What "naive" costs you

Now the bill. Modelling each feature with its own independent Gaussian has a
sharp geometric consequence: each class's cloud is forced to be an
**axis-aligned** ellipse. It can be wide or tall, but never *tilted* — because
a tilt is precisely a correlation between features, and an independent model
has, by construction, zero correlation. Naive Bayes literally cannot draw a
diagonal cloud.

<NaiveAssumption />

At $\rho = 0$ the data really is axis-aligned and naive Bayes' upright ellipse
fits it perfectly. Turn up the correlation and the true cloud tilts while
naive Bayes' fitted ellipse stays stubbornly upright — the two peel apart, and
that gap is the lie made visible. Its probabilities grow untrustworthy and its
boundary bends away from the ideal. A model that *could* see correlation (a
full-covariance Gaussian) would track the tilt; naive Bayes trades that
fidelity for the enormous convenience of §4. Hold onto this picture — in §10
we will find, surprisingly, that the bent boundary is often still good enough
to decide correctly.

## 7. Multinomial naive Bayes and text

Where naive Bayes genuinely shines is text, and the model changes shape to fit
it. Represent a document as a **bag of words** — just the count of each
vocabulary word, order thrown away. Model each class as a weighted bag: a
probability $P(w \mid y)$ for drawing each word $w$, estimated by counting how
often $w$ appears in that class's training documents. A document's likelihood
is then the product of its words' probabilities, and Bayes' rule does the
rest. This is **multinomial naive Bayes**, and it is the classic spam filter.

Working in logs, the decision becomes a running sum: start from the prior
log-odds, then add, for each word, its **log-likelihood ratio** $\log
\frac{P(w \mid \text{spam})}{P(w \mid \text{ham})}$ — positive if the word
leans spam, negative if it leans ham. Every word is a piece of evidence
dropped onto a scale.

<SpamFilter />

Toggle words and watch the needle move: "money", "free", "winner" shove it
towards spam; "meeting", "project", "report" pull it back towards ham. Each
word's contribution is exactly its log-likelihood ratio, and the total — prior
plus every word — is the log-odds of spam. It is Bayes' rule as a tally sheet,
which is why naive Bayes is so fast and so legible. But notice what happens
when you switch off smoothing and add a word never seen in ham, and read on.

## 8. Smoothing and log-space

Two practical problems stand between the idea and a working filter, and both
have one-line fixes.

The first is the **zero-frequency problem**, which you just triggered. If a
word never appeared in any spam training email, then $P(w \mid \text{spam}) =
0$, and because the likelihood is a *product*, that single zero annihilates the
entire spam probability — one unseen word vetoes all the others, no matter how
spammy. The fix is **Laplace (add-one) smoothing**: pretend you saw every
vocabulary word one extra time in each class,

$$P(w \mid y) = \frac{\text{count}(w, y) + \alpha}{\big(\sum_{w'}\text{count}(w', y)\big) + \alpha\,|V|},$$

with $\alpha = 1$. No probability is ever exactly zero, so no single word can
veto the verdict — it can only tug. That is the difference you saw between a
confident, broken 100% and a sensible answer.

The second is **numerical underflow**. Multiplying thousands of small
probabilities drives the result below the smallest number a computer can hold,
and it rounds to zero. The fix is the one the spam widget already used
silently: work in **log-space**, turning the product into a sum of logarithms.
Adding ten thousand modest negative numbers is perfectly stable, and — bonus —
turns the whole classifier into the running tally of §7.

## 9. Generative vs discriminative

Naive Bayes and the logistic regression of Chapter 7 are a famous pair: the
**generative** and **discriminative** approaches to the *same* linear-ish
boundary. In fact, under Gaussian naive Bayes' own assumptions, the posterior
works out to be exactly the sigmoid of logistic regression — the two models
are reaching for the same decision surface from opposite directions. One
estimates each class's distribution and inverts with Bayes' rule; the other
optimises the boundary directly.

The difference shows when their assumptions diverge.

<GenVsDisc />

Here the two classes have very different spreads, so the Bayes-optimal
boundary is a closed *curve*. Gaussian naive Bayes, modelling each class as its
own Gaussian, produces a quadratic boundary that wraps the tight class;
logistic regression, restricted to a straight line, simply cannot. And the
learning curve on the right shows the generative model's other edge: because
it makes more assumptions, naive Bayes squeezes more out of each data point
and pulls ahead, especially when data is scarce. This is the classic result of
Ng and Jordan — the generative model reaches its (sometimes higher) error
floor *faster*. Model the classes, or model the split: two faces of one coin,
and the real lesson of Part V.

## 10. Why a false assumption still works

We have called the independence assumption a lie three times now, so the
obvious question is why naive Bayes works at all — and it does work, routinely
beating fancier models on text. The resolution is subtle and worth holding
onto: **classification only needs the argmax of the posterior to be right, not
the posterior itself.**

The naive assumption badly distorts the *values* of the probabilities. By
treating correlated features as independent witnesses, it double-counts
evidence and grows wildly overconfident — naive Bayes is the model most likely
to tell you it is 99.99% sure when it should say 80% (a direct callback to the
calibration troubles of Chapter 10). But the *ranking* of the classes is far
more robust than the numbers attached to them. As long as the true class still
comes out on top — even by a distorted, overconfident margin — the decision is
correct. The probabilities are not to be trusted; the verdict often can be.

**Complexity** seals the appeal. Training is a single pass over the data to
count and average: $O(np)$ for $n$ points and $p$ features, with no iteration,
no optimisation, nothing to tune but the smoothing constant. Prediction is
$O(pk)$ — a sum of log-terms per class. It is about as cheap as machine
learning gets, trivially parallel, and naturally **online**: a new email just
updates a few counts. Fast, frugal, and good enough — the definition of a
baseline worth beating.

# Two 2-D classes — Gaussian naive Bayes should nail these.
def make_blobs(n, seed, sep=2.0, sd=0.7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    y = np.arange(n) % 2
    cx = np.where(y == 0, -sep, sep)
    return np.stack([rng.normal(cx, sd), rng.normal(0, sd, n)], axis=1), y

def fit_gnb(X, y):
    classes = np.unique(y)
    prior = np.array([(y == c).mean() for c in classes])
    mean  = np.array([X[y == c].mean(0) for c in classes])
    var   = np.array([X[y == c].var(0) + 1e-9 for c in classes])   # tiny floor
    return classes, prior, mean, var

    # sum of log-Gaussians per feature, plus the log prior
    log_lik = (-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * var) - (x - mean) ** 2 / (2 * var)).sum(1)
    return np.log(prior) + log_lik

def gnb_predict(model, X):
    return model[0][np.array([gnb_log_posterior(model, x).argmax() for x in X])]

X, y = make_blobs(300, 1)
model = fit_gnb(X, y)
acc = (gnb_predict(model, X) == y).mean()
print(f"Gaussian NB accuracy: {acc:.3f}   (prior-only baseline: 0.500)")

vocab = ['free', 'money', 'offer', 'meeting', 'project', 'report']
docs = np.array([           # word counts per document
    [2, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0], [1, 2, 1, 0, 0, 1], [2, 1, 2, 1, 0, 0],   # spam
    [0, 1, 0, 2, 1, 1], [1, 0, 1, 1, 2, 1], [0, 1, 0, 2, 1, 2],   # ham
])

def fit_mnb(docs, labels, alpha=1.0):
    V = docs.shape[1]
    log_prior, log_lik = [], []
    for c in [0, 1]:
        log_prior.append(np.log((labels == c).mean()))
        counts = docs[labels == c].sum(0)
        log_lik.append(np.log((counts + alpha) / (counts.sum() + alpha * V)))
    return np.array(log_prior), np.array(log_lik)

log_prior, log_lik = fit_mnb(docs, labels)
def classify(counts):
    score = log_prior + log_lik @ counts
    return 'SPAM' if score[1] > score[0] else 'HAM'

## 12. Problems

### Problem 1 — Base rates and the generative idea

A conceptual question. (a) A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test is 99%
accurate in both directions (99% of sick people test positive; 99% of healthy
people test negative). You test positive — what is the probability you are
actually sick? Use Bayes' rule, and explain why the answer is so far from 99%.
(b) What does it mean to call naive Bayes a *generative* classifier, as opposed
to logistic regression's *discriminative*? (c) State the naive assumption
precisely and give one concrete case where it is plainly false.

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** Let $S$ be sick, $+$ a positive test. $P(S) = 0.001$, $P(+\mid S) =
0.99$, $P(+\mid \neg S) = 0.01$. By Bayes' rule,
$$P(S \mid +) = \frac{P(+\mid S)P(S)}{P(+\mid S)P(S) + P(+\mid \neg S)P(\neg S)} = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.01 \times 0.999} \approx 0.090.$$
About **9%** — not 99%. The test's accuracy is the *likelihood* $P(+\mid S)$,
but the answer you want is the *posterior* $P(S \mid +)$, and the prior
$P(S)$ is tiny. Because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones, the 1% of
them who test positive (false positives) swamp the 99% of the few sick people
who do. The base rate dominates.

**(b)** A *generative* classifier models how each class produces data, $P(x
\mid y)$, and inverts with Bayes' rule to get $P(y \mid x)$; you could even
*sample* synthetic data from it. A *discriminative* classifier models the
boundary $P(y \mid x)$ directly and cannot generate data. Naive Bayes is
generative; logistic regression and the SVM are discriminative.

**(c)** The naive assumption: given the class, the features are mutually
independent, $P(x \mid y) = \prod_j P(x_j \mid y)$. Plainly false example: in
spam, "free" and "money" co-occur far more than independence predicts; in
images, neighbouring pixels are strongly correlated.

</details>

n_spam, n_ham = 40, 60
p_free = {'spam': 30/40, 'ham': 6/60}
p_meet = {'spam': 4/40,  'ham': 45/60}
prior = {'spam': n_spam/100, 'ham': n_ham/100}

def posterior(free, meet):
    # free, meet are 1 (present) or 0 (absent)
    lik = {}
    for c in ['spam', 'ham']:
        pf = p_free[c] if free else (1 - p_free[c])
        pm = p_meet[c] if meet else (1 - p_meet[c])
        lik[c] = prior[c] * pf * pm
    z = lik['spam'] + lik['ham']
    return lik['spam'] / z

print(f"P(spam | free & meeting) = {posterior(1, 1):.3f}")
print(f"P(spam | free only)      = {posterior(1, 0):.3f}")
print(f"P(spam | meeting only)   = {posterior(0, 1):.3f}")

— a strongly hammy word — and the posterior collapses back below ½: the two
independent pieces of evidence pull in opposite directions and naive Bayes
multiplies them. Each feature is its own witness; the verdict is their product.

Naive Bayes stays ahead at every size. Its generative model knows the classes
have different spreads, so it draws a *curved* boundary that hugs the tight

---

Naive Bayes is the bargain of machine learning: one pass over the data to
count and average, an assumption everyone knows is false, and in return a
classifier that trains in a blink, runs on a phone, needs barely any data, and
embarrasses far fancier models on text. It earns its keep not by being right
about the world — its independence assumption is a fiction and its confident
probabilities are not to be trusted — but by being right about the *decision*
often enough to matter. It is the baseline you should beat before you believe
anything more complicated is helping.
