# The perceptron and multilayer perceptrons

*From a single artificial neuron to a network of them — and the moment a model
stops fitting the features you give it and starts building its own.*

Every model in this book so far has had its cleverness handed to it. Linear and
logistic regression draw a line because you told them the features; the support
vector machine bends that line only through a kernel you chose; the decision tree
splits on columns you supplied; even principal components are just a rotation of
the axes you already had. When the data needed a curve, it was *you* who built
the curve — the squared term, the interaction, the kernel, the polynomial. The
model only ever fit weights to features a human designed.

The artificial neuron is where that changes. It begins almost insultingly
simple — a caricature of a brain cell that multiplies each of its inputs by a
weight, adds them up, and fires if the total crosses a threshold — and on its own
it is nothing you have not seen: a single neuron is a straight line, the logistic
regression of Chapter 7 wearing biological clothes. The magic is not in the
neuron. It is in what happens when you stack them. Wire the outputs of one layer
of neurons into the inputs of another, put a simple non-linearity between them,
and the network stops merely fitting features and starts *building* them —
manufacturing, on its own, exactly the curved coordinates that you used to have
to hand-craft.

That is the whole idea of deep learning, in one sentence, and this chapter is
where it begins. You will watch a single perceptron learn a line and then thrash
helplessly against the XOR problem that no line can solve; you will stack a
handful of neurons into a hidden layer and watch the same problem fall; and you
will see, in the hidden layer's own coordinates, the network quietly reshaping
the data until a straight line is all the answer needs. How it learns those
weights — the chain rule turned into an algorithm — is the next chapter. This one
is about what it can build.

## 1. A neuron

The whole edifice rests on one tiny unit, borrowed loosely from biology. A real
neuron collects electrical signals from thousands of others through its
dendrites, sums them, and fires a pulse down its axon only if the total exceeds
some threshold. Strip that to arithmetic and you have the **artificial neuron**:
take the inputs $x_1, \dots, x_d$, multiply each by a **weight** $w_i$ (how much
that input matters, and in which direction), add a **bias** $b$ (the threshold,
moved to the other side), and pass the weighted sum through an **activation
function** $\phi$:

$$\text{output} = \phi\!\left(\sum_i w_i x_i + b\right) = \phi(w \cdot x + b).$$

That is the entire unit. The weights and bias are what the neuron *learns*; the
activation is a fixed non-linearity we choose. Everything in this chapter — and,
scaled up a billionfold, everything in modern AI — is made of this one part,
wired together. So it is worth meeting it alone first.

## 2. The perceptron

The earliest and simplest neuron, Rosenblatt's 1958 **perceptron**, uses the
bluntest possible activation: a **step**. Compute $z = w \cdot x + b$, and fire 1
if $z \ge 0$, else 0. Geometrically the equation $w \cdot x + b = 0$ is a line
(in 2-D) or a hyperplane (in general), with the weights $w$ as its normal
direction; everything on one side outputs 1 and everything on the other outputs
0. A single perceptron is therefore exactly a **linear classifier**.

<SingleNeuron />

Drag the weights and the boundary tilts; drag the bias and it slides. Switch the
activation from the hard `step` to the smooth `sigmoid` and the crisp edge melts
into a gradient — and at that moment the neuron becomes something you already
know intimately. A single sigmoid neuron computes $\sigma(w \cdot x + b)$, which
is precisely the **logistic regression** of Chapter 7. The neuron is not a new
model; it is the old one, renamed as a building block and waiting to be stacked.

## 3. The perceptron learning rule

How does a perceptron find its line? With the first genuine *learning* algorithm
in the book, and a beautifully simple one. Show the perceptron a training point.
If it classifies the point correctly, change nothing. If it gets it wrong, nudge
the weights a little in the direction that would have helped:

$$w \leftarrow w + \eta\,(y - \hat{y})\,x, \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\,(y - \hat{y}),$$

where $\hat{y}$ is the perceptron's guess, $y$ the truth, and $\eta$ a small step
size. If the truth was 1 and it guessed 0, the weights shift toward the point; if
the other way, away from it. Cycle through the data, fixing mistakes, until there
are none left.

<PerceptronLearning />

On linearly separable data this is guaranteed to work: the **perceptron
convergence theorem** proves that if a separating line exists, the rule will find
one in a finite number of steps — watch the boundary rotate into place and stop.
The guarantee is real, and for a while it made the perceptron look like the key
to artificial thought. Then someone asked it to learn XOR.

## 4. The wall: XOR

Switch the widget above to **XOR** and watch the algorithm come apart. The line
lurches back and forth, fixing one point only to break another, and never
settles — because there is nothing to settle on. XOR is the checkerboard pattern
of Chapter 17: class 1 in two opposite corners, class 0 in the other two, and
*no straight line separates them*. A single perceptron can only ever draw a
straight boundary, so it cannot represent XOR, and the convergence theorem simply
does not apply — there is no separating line for it to find.

This is not a quirk of one toy problem. It is the fundamental limit of a single
neuron, and when Minsky and Papert laid it out in 1969 it was devastating: if a
perceptron cannot learn something as trivial as XOR, what hope for anything real?
Funding dried up, and the field went quiet for over a decade — the first "AI
winter". The irony is that the escape was already in plain sight, and almost
embarrassingly simple. You just need more than one neuron.

## 5. Stacking neurons: the multilayer perceptron

Here is the fix. Instead of one neuron mapping inputs straight to an output, put a
**hidden layer** of several neurons in between. Each hidden neuron takes the same
inputs but with its own weights, so each draws its own line and computes its own
0-to-1 signal. Then a final **output neuron** takes *those* signals as *its*
inputs and combines them into the answer. Inputs feed the hidden layer; the
hidden layer feeds the output. That is a **multilayer perceptron** (MLP), the
simplest neural network.

The point is what the hidden layer does. Its neurons are not classifying the
data; they are manufacturing new **features** — each one a learned, intermediate
question about the input ("is the point up and to the right?"). The output neuron
then draws a straight boundary not in the raw input space, where no line works,
but in the space of those new features, where one does. For XOR you need only two
hidden neurons: one fires for the top-right region, one for the bottom-left, and
the output combines them. Stacking has turned an impossible problem into a linear
one — but only because of a detail we have glossed over: the activation in
between.

## 6. Why non-linearity is the whole point

It is tempting to think the power comes from stacking, but stacking alone buys
nothing. Suppose the activation were the identity — just pass the weighted sum
through unchanged. Then each layer applies a linear transformation, and a linear
transformation of a linear transformation is *still linear*: the whole deep
network collapses into a single equivalent linear layer, no more expressive than
one neuron. A thousand stacked linear layers still draw only a straight line.

<ActivationZoo />

The **activation function** is the non-linear bend between the layers that
prevents the collapse, and it is what lets depth build complexity. The classic
choices saturate — the **sigmoid** ($0$ to $1$) and **tanh** ($-1$ to $1$)
flatten out at their extremes — and that flattening, as the dashed derivatives
show, is a problem we will meet in Chapter 22: where the curve is flat the
gradient is nearly zero and learning crawls. The modern default, **ReLU**
($\max(0, z)$), and its **leaky** variant fix this with a simple hinge that stays
steep, which is why nearly every deep network today is built from them. The
bottom panel makes the stakes concrete: the same six-unit network draws a useless
straight line with a linear activation and bends to solve XOR the moment you give
it ReLU.

## 7. Forward propagation

With the pieces named, the computation a network performs is just a chain of the
same three operations, layer by layer. Collect a layer's weights into a matrix
$W^{(l)}$ and its biases into a vector $b^{(l)}$; then the layer's output is

$$a^{(l)} = \phi\!\left(W^{(l)} a^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)}\right),$$

a **matrix multiply** (every neuron's weighted sum at once), an **added bias**,
and an **activation**, applied to the previous layer's output. Start with the
input $a^{(0)} = x$, apply this for each layer in turn, and the final layer emits
the prediction. That is **forward propagation** — literally all a trained network
does to turn an input into an output. A modern network with billions of
parameters is doing exactly this, just with very large matrices and very many
layers. The only remaining mystery is where the numbers in $W$ and $b$ come from,
and that is Chapter 22.

## 8. Solving XOR, and any shape

Let it run. Below, a small MLP trains live — press Play and watch the decision
boundary form out of nothing as the weights adjust.

<MLPBoundary />

With a single hidden unit the network can only draw a straight line and XOR stays
broken; raise the hidden-unit count and the boundary springs to life, wrapping
itself around the checkerboard, the moons, the rings. Each faint grey line is one
hidden neuron's own boundary, and the output neuron blends them — every added
unit contributes another fold the boundary can use. This is the kernel trick of
Chapter 15 and the basis expansion of Chapter 17 made flesh: the network is
lifting the data into a richer space where a straight cut works. The difference,
and it is the difference that defines deep learning, is that *no one designed the
lift*. The network found it.

## 9. Hidden layers learn features

That last point deserves to be seen directly, because it is the conceptual heart
of the entire field. A hidden layer is a **learned change of representation**.

<HiddenSpace />

On the left is XOR in its raw input coordinates, hopelessly tangled — no line
separates the classes. The network has just two hidden units; on the right, each
point is re-plotted in *their* coordinates $(h_1, h_2)$ — what the hidden layer
sees instead of the raw input. Press Play, and as the network trains it warps
that hidden space, stretching and folding until the two classes pull cleanly
apart and a single straight line, the output neuron, slices between them. The
network has *learned* the exact lift that the kernel trick had to be handed and
the basis expansion had to be hand-built. This is what people mean when they say
neural networks "learn representations", and it is why stacking more layers —
each transforming the representation of the one below — is so powerful. Depth is
representation upon representation.

## 10. How big a network? Universal approximation, width, and depth

How much can a network represent? Remarkably, almost anything. The **universal
approximation theorem** states that a network with a *single* hidden layer, given
enough units, can approximate any continuous function to any desired accuracy. So
expressiveness is never the wall — in principle one hidden layer suffices for
everything.

In practice it is not so simple, and the gap is the reason for the word "deep".
"Enough units" can be astronomically, uselessly many for a shallow network forced
to memorise a complicated function in one layer. A **deep** network — many layers
stacked — instead builds complex features out of simpler ones, edges into shapes
into objects, and reaches the same expressiveness with exponentially fewer units.
Depth is an efficiency of representation, not of raw power. *Complexity*: a
forward pass is a handful of matrix multiplies, costing on the order of the number
of weights per example — cheap to run, which is why a trained network predicts
in microseconds even when training it took a fortune.

## 11. Implementing it yourself

A two-layer MLP forward pass, in a few lines — and proof that the right weights
solve XOR. (Where those weights *come from* is the next chapter; here we set them
by hand.)

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return np.where(z >= 0, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z)), np.exp(z) / (1 + np.exp(z)))

# A 2 -> 2 -> 1 network. The hidden units compute OR and AND of the inputs;
# the output computes "OR and NOT AND" — which is exactly XOR.
W1 = np.array([[20.0, 20.0],   # hidden unit 1: fires if x1 OR x2
               [20.0, 20.0]])
b1 = np.array([-10.0, -30.0])  # ...unit 2: fires only if x1 AND x2
W2 = np.array([[20.0, -20.0]]) # output: hidden1 AND NOT hidden2
b2 = np.array([-10.0])

def forward(x):
    h = sigmoid(W1 @ x + b1)        # hidden layer: matmul, bias, activation
    return sigmoid(W2 @ h + b2)[0]  # output layer: the same three steps

print("input -> output (target = XOR)")
for x1 in (0, 1):
    for x2 in (0, 1):
        out = forward(np.array([x1, x2], float))
        print(f"  ({x1},{x2}) -> {out:.3f}   target {x1 ^ x2}")
`}
/>

The forward pass is nothing but matrix-multiply, add-bias, activate, repeated per
layer — and with these hand-chosen weights the 2-2-1 network produces clean 0s
and 1s in the XOR pattern that no single neuron could. Scaling this up is mostly a
matter of bigger matrices; the hard part, learning the weights instead of
guessing them, is Chapter 22.

## 12. Problems

### Problem 1 — Neurons, lines, and layers

A conceptual question. (a) Why is a single perceptron only ever a linear
classifier? (b) Why can it not learn XOR, and what specifically does adding a
hidden layer give you that fixes it? (c) Why does a network with the identity
(linear) activation gain *nothing* from extra layers, no matter how many units?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** A perceptron outputs $\phi(w\cdot x + b)$, and its decision flips exactly
where $w\cdot x + b = 0$ — the equation of a line (or hyperplane). Everything on
one side is one class, everything on the other is the other. There is no way to
bend that surface; it is straight by construction.

**(b)** XOR places the two classes in opposite corners of a square, and no
straight line can separate opposite corners from the other two — so a single
linear boundary cannot represent it. A hidden layer adds intermediate neurons
that each draw their own line and build new *features*; the output neuron then
draws a straight boundary in the space of those features, where the problem *is*
linearly separable. The non-linearity in between is what makes those features
more than a disguised linear combination.

**(c)** With the identity activation each layer is a linear map, and the
composition of linear maps is itself a single linear map ($W_2(W_1 x) = (W_2 W_1)
x$). So a stack of linear layers is exactly equivalent to one linear layer — it
can still only draw a straight boundary. Depth buys expressiveness *only* because
a non-linear activation sits between the layers.

</details>

H = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)

print("linear classifier on HIDDEN activations:", round((fit_logreg(H, y)(H) == y).mean(), 3))
print("linear classifier on RAW inputs:        ", round((fit_logreg(X, y)(X) == y).mean(), 3))

/>

A linear classifier is helpless on the raw XOR inputs (~50%, no better than a
coin) but near-perfect on the hidden activations. The network has transformed the
data into a representation where the classes *are* linearly separable — it learned
the kernel trick, the lift you used to build by hand. That single fact —
representations can be learned — is the whole reason the next ten years of this
field happened.

The multilayer perceptron is the hinge of this book. Behind it lie twenty
chapters of models that drew their boundaries from a formula, a margin, a split,
or a rotation — every one of them fitting weights to features that a person had to
design. Ahead lies everything that calls itself deep learning, and all of it is
this same move repeated and scaled: stack simple units, put a non-linearity
between them, and let the network manufacture its own representations layer upon
layer. The neuron is a humble atom — a weighted sum and a bend — but composed in
the thousands and the millions it has learned to recognise faces, translate
languages, and fold proteins.

We have built the forward pass — what a network *is*, and what it can represent —
but quietly assumed the one thing that makes it useful: that it can find the right
weights from data. With a million of them tangled across a dozen layers, how could
you possibly compute which way to nudge each one?
