# A mathematical toolkit

*The small amount of mathematics the book actually uses — vectors, slopes, and a
little probability — taught as pictures, and only when you need it.*

Machine learning has a reputation for demanding heavy mathematics, and for the
people inventing new algorithms it does. But for *understanding* the algorithms in
this book — for seeing why they work and what they are doing — the required maths is
modest, and almost all of it is geometric. Three small toolkits cover it: a little
**linear algebra** (vectors and dot products, the language of the data and the
models), a little **calculus** (slopes and gradients, the fuel of optimisation), and
a little **probability** (distributions and Bayes' rule, the language of noise and
belief).

This chapter introduces just those pieces, each as a picture you can drag, and each
tagged to the exact later chapter where it pays off. The goal is not rigour — there
are no proofs here — but *fluency*: enough comfort with these few ideas that when
$w \cdot x$ or $\nabla L$ or $P(y \mid x)$ appears later, it reads as a familiar
shape rather than a wall of symbols. If you already know this material, skim it and
move on; if you do not, ten minutes here will save you hours later.

## 1. Why a little maths

It is worth saying what each toolkit is *for*, so the abstraction stays anchored.
**Linear algebra** is how we represent data and models. A single example — an email,
a house, a patient — is a list of numbers, which we treat as a *vector*; a whole
dataset is a stack of them, a *matrix*; and the simplest models combine a vector of
inputs with a vector of weights using one operation, the dot product. **Calculus** is
how models *learn*: the derivative measures how a small change in a parameter changes
the loss, the gradient bundles those measurements into a direction, and following it
downhill is how nearly every model in the book is trained. **Probability** is how we
handle the fact that data is *noisy*: distributions describe the noise, expectation
summarises it, and Bayes' rule updates beliefs in light of evidence.

Three tools, three jobs: represent the data, train the model, handle the noise. Take
them one at a time.

## 2. Vectors

A **vector** is just an ordered list of numbers — $[3, 1, 4]$ — and it wears two hats
at once. Geometrically, it is a *point* in space (three numbers locate a point in
three dimensions) or, equivalently, an *arrow* from the origin to that point. In
machine learning, a vector is almost always one **example**: the features of a house
$[\text{bedrooms}, \text{area}, \text{age}]$ are a point in a three-dimensional
"house space", and a dataset of houses is a cloud of such points. Learning is then a
geometric act — drawing a boundary through the cloud, finding its centre, measuring
distances between points.

Vectors add tip-to-tail and scale by stretching, but the operation that matters most
for us combines two of them into a single number, and it has a beautiful geometric
meaning.

## 3. The dot product

The **dot product** of two vectors multiplies them component-by-component and adds
the results: $a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots$. That definition looks dry; its
meaning is not.

<DotProduct />

The dot product measures **how much two vectors point in the same direction**.
Equivalently, $a \cdot b = |a|\,|b|\cos\theta$ — the lengths multiplied by the cosine
of the angle between them — so it is large and positive when the vectors align, drops
to exactly zero when they are perpendicular, and goes negative when they oppose. The
grey shadow in the widget is the *projection* of one vector onto the other: the dot
product is the length of that shadow, scaled.

This is the single most important operation in the book, because **every linear model
scores an input with a dot product.** The "weighted sum" $w \cdot x = w_1 x_1 + w_2
x_2 + \dots$ at the heart of linear regression, logistic regression, the perceptron,
and the support vector machine is exactly this: it asks how strongly the input $x$
aligns with the learned weight vector $w$. Big positive dot product, strong "yes";
big negative, strong "no". When you meet $w \cdot x$ later, picture this alignment.

## 4. Matrices transform space

Stack vectors into a grid and you have a **matrix**. Matrices do two jobs in the
book. First, they *hold data*: a dataset of $n$ examples with $d$ features each is an
$n \times d$ matrix, one row per example. Second, and more deeply, a matrix
*transforms space*: multiplying a vector by a matrix rotates, stretches, shears, or
projects it into a new set of coordinates. A whole layer of a neural network is one
such transformation — a matrix multiply that reshapes the data — and the principal
components of Chapter 20 are a matrix that rotates the data onto its most informative
axes. You will not need to multiply matrices by hand here; you only need the mental
image: **a matrix is a machine that reshapes the space the data lives in.** The dot
product is the atom of that operation — each entry of a matrix–vector product is one
dot product.

## 5. The derivative

Now calculus, which is how models learn. The **derivative** of a function $f$ at a
point is the *slope* of its graph there — the rate at which the output changes as you
nudge the input, written $f'(x)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}$.

The slope carries two pieces of information we will lean on constantly: its **sign**
says which way the function is heading (positive slope, rising as $x$ increases;
negative, falling), and its **magnitude** says how steeply. Crucially, where a smooth
function reaches a minimum or maximum, its slope is **zero** — the tangent is flat.
That fact is the entire basis of optimisation: to minimise a loss, find where its
slope is zero, and to get there, keep stepping in the downhill direction the slope
points out. We see exactly that next.

## 6. The gradient

Most losses depend on many parameters, not one, so we need the multi-dimensional
version of the slope: the **gradient**, written $\nabla f$.

<SlopeAndGradient />

On the left, the derivative in one dimension is the slope of the tangent line — drag
the point and watch it tilt, going flat exactly at the hump and the dip. On the
right, the gradient in two dimensions is an *arrow*: it collects the slope along each
axis into a single direction that points **straight uphill** — perpendicular to the
contour lines (the curves of constant height) and longest where the surface is
steepest. Its defining property is that it points in the direction of fastest
increase.

Which is exactly what we want, run backwards. To *minimise* a loss, we compute its
gradient and step in the **opposite** direction — straight downhill, the fastest way
down — then recompute and step again. That is **gradient descent**, the optimiser
behind nearly every model in this book, and the whole subject of Chapter 6. The
gradient is the compass; the chapters after this one are about following it well.

## 7. Randomness and distributions

The third toolkit handles the fact that real data is noisy — two houses with
identical features sell for different prices; the same measurement taken twice gives
two readings. We model that scatter with **probability distributions**, which assign
a likelihood to each possible value, and one distribution dominates the book.

<DistributionExplorer />

The **Gaussian** (or normal) distribution — the bell curve — is the default model of
noise and uncertainty everywhere in machine learning, partly because sums of many
small random effects tend toward it. Two numbers describe it completely: the **mean**
$\mu$, the centre the values cluster around, and the **standard deviation** $\sigma$
(or its square, the **variance** $\sigma^2$), the spread. Drag them and the bell
slides and stretches over the samples it generated. When a later chapter says a
dataset is "a line plus Gaussian noise", or that logistic regression and naive Bayes
rest on likelihoods, this bell is what is meant.

## 8. Mean, variance, and expectation

Two summaries of a distribution recur so often they deserve a name in passing. The
**mean** (or **expectation**, $\mathbb{E}[X]$) is the average value — the centre of
mass of the distribution, the long-run average if you sampled forever. The
**variance** is the average squared distance from the mean — a measure of spread, and
the reason "minimise the squared error" is the natural loss for Gaussian noise. These
two ideas quietly run through the book: the bias–variance trade-off of Chapter 2 is
about them, the principal components of Chapter 20 are the directions of greatest
variance, and the "expected loss" an optimiser minimises is an expectation. You do
not need their formulas at your fingertips, only the intuition: **mean is where,
variance is how spread out.**

## 9. Bayes' rule

The last tool is for *updating beliefs* with evidence, and it is a single equation:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}.$$

Read it as: the probability of $A$ *given that you observed* $B$ equals how well $A$
predicts $B$, times how likely $A$ was to begin with, divided by how likely $B$ was
overall. It is the mathematics of evidence — start with a prior belief, see some
data, end with an updated belief — and it is the entire engine of the naive Bayes
classifier (Chapter 16) and the probabilistic reading of logistic regression
(Chapter 7). Its most famous lesson is that rare things stay rare even after
suggestive evidence: a very accurate test for a very rare disease still yields mostly
false alarms, because the prior $P(\text{disease})$ is so small. Bayes' rule is how
you reason correctly about that, and we will use it in earnest later.

## 10. Where each tool returns

That is the whole toolkit, and every piece has a forwarding address. **Dot products**
are the weighted sums of Chapters 5, 7, 8, 15, and 21. **Matrices** are the data
itself, the transformations of neural-network layers (Chapter 21), and the rotation
of PCA (Chapter 20). The **gradient** is the engine of Chapter 6 and, through it,
nearly all of training, climaxing in backpropagation (Chapter 22). The **Gaussian**
is the noise in every regression and the likelihood in Chapters 7 and 16; **mean and
variance** underlie the bias–variance trade-off and PCA; and **Bayes' rule** powers
naive Bayes and the probabilistic view of classification. None of it is introduced
for its own sake. Each tool is a thing you will pick up and use, and now you know
what it is for.

## 11. Problems

### Problem 1 — Dot products by hand and by meaning

A short exercise. For $a = [3, 4]$ and several $b$: (a) compute $a \cdot b$ for
$b = [4, -3]$, and say what the result tells you about the angle between them; (b)
find a non-zero $b$ that is *perpendicular* to $a$; (c) of $b = [1, 1]$ and $b = [-1,
-1]$, which aligns with $a$ and which opposes it, and how do you know from the dot
product?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** $a \cdot b = 3\cdot4 + 4\cdot(-3) = 12 - 12 = 0$. A zero dot product means the
vectors are **perpendicular** ($\cos\theta = 0$, so $\theta = 90°$). Indeed $[3,4]$
and $[4,-3]$ are at right angles.

**(b)** Any scalar multiple of $[4, -3]$ works — e.g. $b = [4, -3]$ or $[-8, 6]$ —
since $a \cdot [4,-3] = 0$. (Swap the components and negate one: $[3,4] \to
[4,-3]$.)

**(c)** $a \cdot [1,1] = 3 + 4 = 7 > 0$, so $[1,1]$ **aligns** with $a$ (acute angle).
$a \cdot [-1,-1] = -3 - 4 = -7 < 0$, so $[-1,-1]$ **opposes** $a$ (obtuse angle). The
*sign* of the dot product alone tells you which side of perpendicular two vectors are
on — exactly how a linear classifier turns $w\cdot x$ into a yes/no decision.

</details>

### Problem 2 — A dot product is a weighted sum

Connect the geometry to a model. A simple credit score weights three features —
income, debt, and age — with weights $w = [0.5, -0.8, 0.1]$. Compute the score
$w \cdot x$ for an applicant $x = [6, 2, 3]$, and explain what the signs of the
weights mean.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

w = np.array([0.5, -0.8, 0.1])   # weights for [income, debt, age]
x = np.array([6.0, 2.0, 3.0])    # an applicant

# Your code:
#   1. compute the score as the dot product w . x  (try w @ x)
#   2. explain why a positive weight helps the score and a negative one hurts it
`}
/>

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

w = np.array([0.5, -0.8, 0.1])
x = np.array([6.0, 2.0, 3.0])

score = w @ x          # = 0.5*6 + (-0.8)*2 + 0.1*3
print("score:", score)
print("contributions:", w * x)   # each feature's push on the score
`}
/>

The score is $0.5(6) - 0.8(2) + 0.1(3) = 3 - 1.6 + 0.3 = 1.7$. A **positive** weight
(income, age) means more of that feature *raises* the score; a **negative** weight
(debt) means more of it *lowers* the score. The dot product is just the sum of these
signed contributions — and learning a linear model is nothing but finding the weight
vector whose dot product with the inputs best separates the outcomes.

</details>

### Problem 3 — Compute a gradient

The gradient points uphill; check it numerically. For $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$,
compute the gradient at a point both analytically and by finite differences, and
confirm they match.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def f(x, y):
    return x**2 + 3*y**2

# Your code:
#   1. the analytic gradient is (df/dx, df/dy) = (2x, 6y); evaluate it at (1.5, 0.9)
#   2. estimate it by finite differences: (f(x+h,y)-f(x-h,y))/(2h) for each axis
#   3. confirm the two agree, and note the gradient points away from the minimum
`}
/>

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def f(x, y):
    return x**2 + 3*y**2

x, y, h = 1.5, 0.9, 1e-5
analytic = np.array([2*x, 6*y])
finite = np.array([(f(x+h, y) - f(x-h, y)) / (2*h),
                   (f(x, y+h) - f(x, y-h)) / (2*h)])
print("analytic gradient:", analytic.round(4))
print("finite-difference:", finite.round(4))
print("points away from the minimum (0,0):", analytic)   # both components have the sign of x, y
`}
/>

Both give $(3, 5.4)$. The gradient points *uphill* — away from the minimum at the
origin — so to descend the function you step the other way, in the direction of
$-\nabla f$. The finite-difference check is exactly how you verify any gradient you
compute by hand, the same trick used to validate backpropagation in Chapter 22.

</details>

### Problem 4 — Mean and variance of samples

Draw samples from a Gaussian and confirm the sample mean and variance recover its
parameters as the sample grows.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma = 2.0, 1.5

# Your code:
#   1. draw 50 samples from a normal with this mean and standard deviation
#   2. print the sample mean and sample variance
#   3. repeat with 50000 samples and watch them converge to mu and sigma**2
`}
/>

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma = 2.0, 1.5

for n in [50, 50000]:
    s = rng.normal(mu, sigma, n)
    print(f"n={n:>6}: mean {s.mean():.3f} (-> {mu}),  variance {s.var():.3f} (-> {sigma**2})")
`}
/>

With 50 samples the estimates are roughly right but noisy; with 50000 they lock onto
$\mu = 2$ and $\sigma^2 = 2.25$. This is the law of large numbers in action — averages
of more data converge on the true expectation — and it is the quiet justification for
the entire enterprise: more examples give a better picture of the underlying
distribution.

</details>

### Problem 5 — Bayes' rule and the rare disease

The classic. A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test is 99% accurate in both
directions. You test positive — what is the probability you actually have the
disease?

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`# A conceptual + arithmetic problem.
# P(disease) = 0.001
# P(positive | disease) = 0.99       (true positive rate)
# P(positive | healthy) = 0.01       (false positive rate)
#
# Your code: use Bayes' rule to compute P(disease | positive). Is it close to 99%?
`}
/>

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`p_disease = 0.001
p_pos_given_disease = 0.99
p_pos_given_healthy = 0.01

# P(positive) = P(pos|disease)P(disease) + P(pos|healthy)P(healthy)
p_pos = p_pos_given_disease*p_disease + p_pos_given_healthy*(1 - p_disease)
# Bayes' rule:
p_disease_given_pos = p_pos_given_disease * p_disease / p_pos
print(f"P(disease | positive) = {p_disease_given_pos:.3f}")
`}
/>

The answer is about **0.090** — only 9%, nowhere near 99%. The test's accuracy is
$P(\text{positive} \mid \text{disease})$, but the question asks for $P(\text{disease}
\mid \text{positive})$, and Bayes' rule shows these differ enormously when the disease
is rare: the healthy population is so large that its 1% false positives swamp the few
true positives. This base-rate reasoning is the heart of the naive Bayes classifier in
Chapter 16, and a mistake people make every day.

</details>
