# What is a language model?

*Strip away the mystique and there is one task underneath everything: predict
the next word. The whole field is what happens when you do that absurdly well.*

Your phone has done it for years. You type *I'll be there in five*, and it
offers *minutes*. That is a language model — a system that, given some text, puts
a probability on what comes next. The thing in your pocket is small and weak; the
thing behind a modern chatbot is enormous and strong. But the task is *identical*.
A large language model is autocomplete that read most of the internet.

That is not a deflation — it is the whole secret. Everything an LLM appears to do
— answer questions, write code, translate, reason step by step — it does by
predicting next tokens, one at a time, extremely well. There is no separate
"understanding" module bolted on. Understanding, to whatever degree it exists, is
a *side effect* of getting very good at one narrow game.

By the end of this chapter you will know exactly what that game is, why playing it
well forces a model to learn far more than spelling, and you will have built the
simplest possible language model with your own hands. Everything in the rest of
the book is an answer to the question: *how do you get good at next-token
prediction?*

## 1. The one objective

Fix a vocabulary of tokens (Chapter 2 makes "token" precise; for now, read it as
"word"). A language model is a function that takes a sequence of tokens — the
**context** — and returns a probability distribution over which token comes next:

$$P(\text{next token} \mid \text{context}).$$

That's the entire specification. Feed it *The capital of France is* and a good
model puts most of its probability on *Paris*. Feed it *Once upon a time, there
was a* and the probability spreads across hundreds of plausible nouns, because
genuinely many words could come next. The model isn't choosing a word yet — it is
reporting its beliefs about all of them at once.

Here is a real one doing exactly that. This is GPT-2's actual next-token
distribution for a few contexts — no tricks, the model's own probabilities.

## 2. Predicting is classifying

If this feels familiar from the first book, it should. Predicting the next token is
**classification** — the same problem as Chapters 7 and 8 there, just with a
fifty-thousand-way choice instead of two. The model produces a score for every
token in the vocabulary, a softmax turns those scores into probabilities, and
training nudges the weights to put more mass on whatever token actually came next.

This is why the objective is so powerful as a *teacher*: the labels are free. Every
sentence ever written is a stack of training examples — for each position, the
"correct answer" is simply the token that genuinely follows. No human has to label
anything. Point the model at a trillion words of text and it has a trillion
self-supervised examples. This is **pretraining**, and we return to it in
Chapter 10; for now, just notice that the data problem solves itself.

## 3. Why next-token prediction is not trivial

It is tempting to think such a simple objective could only ever learn surface
statistics — which words tend to follow which. And a weak model does exactly that.
But push the task hard enough and something else happens.

To predict the next token in *The murderer turned out to be the* you must track who
appeared in the story and who had motive. To finish *2,341 + 6,809 =* you must
actually add. To continue a proof you must follow the argument. Each of these is
"just" next-token prediction, but doing it *well* is impossible without modelling
the thing the text is about. Compression forces comprehension: the cheapest way to
predict text generated by a world is to build a small model of that world. That is
the bet the whole field is making, and so far it keeps paying off.

## 4. The simplest language model

To make "language model" fully concrete, here is one you can read end to end — a
**bigram model**. It learns nothing but which word tends to follow which, from a
tiny corpus, then generates by sampling. It is hopeless, but it is unmistakably a
language model: context in, distribution over next word out.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import random
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

text = ("the cat sat on the mat . the dog sat on the log . "
        "the cat ran to the dog . the dog ran to the mat .").split()

# Count which word follows which.
counts = defaultdict(Counter)
for a, b in zip(text, text[1:]):
    counts[a][b] += 1

def predict(word):
    "Return P(next | word) as a sorted list."
    c = counts[word]
    total = sum(c.values())
    return sorted(((w, n / total) for w, n in c.items()), key=lambda x: -x[1])

print("after 'the':", predict("the"))
print("after 'sat':", predict("sat"))

# Generate by sampling from the distribution, one token at a time.
random.seed(0)
word, out = "the", ["the"]
for _ in range(12):
    nxt = counts[word]
    if not nxt:
        break
    word = random.choices(list(nxt), weights=list(nxt.values()))[0]
    out.append(word)
print("\\ngenerated:", " ".join(out))
`}
/>

That is the whole idea, in miniature: estimate $P(\text{next} \mid \text{context})$,
then sample from it repeatedly. A transformer replaces the lookup table with a deep
network and the one-word context with thousands of tokens of attention — but the
shape of the task never changes from what you just ran.

## 5. From prediction to a helpful assistant

One loose end. A raw next-token predictor trained on the internet will, given *What
is the capital of France?*, often continue with *What is the capital of Germany?* —
because that's the kind of text that follows a question in the wild (a list of more
questions). It is a brilliant mimic with no instinct to *help*. Turning that mimic
into something that answers when asked takes an extra training stage —
instruction-tuning and learning from human preferences — which is the story of
Chapters 12 and 13. The base model supplies the raw capability; alignment aims it
at you.

### Problem 1 — Confident or unsure?

Using Figure 1.1, find the context where GPT-2 is most confident and the one where
it is least confident. What is it about each context that makes the model certain
or uncertain? Connect your answer to the shape of the distribution.

<details>

*The opposite of hot is* is the most confident — *cold* dominates, because the
context almost determines the answer. *Once upon a time, there was a* is the least
— dozens of nouns are all plausible continuations, so the mass spreads thin.
Confidence is just how peaked the distribution is, and that depends on how much the
context constrains what can come next. A good model is *appropriately* uncertain:
sharp when the answer is forced, broad when it genuinely isn't.

### Problem 2 — The bigram's ceiling

Extend the §4 model to a **trigram** (condition on the previous *two* words instead
of one). Why does this help, and why can't you fix the model's real problem just by
using longer and longer n-grams?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

Conditioning on two words captures a little more structure, so predictions sharpen.
But the table grows exponentially with the context length, and almost every long
context appears zero times in any finite corpus — so the counts collapse to noise.
This is the curse that killed n-gram models and motivated neural ones: you need a
model that *generalises* across contexts it has never seen exactly, by representing
words as vectors rather than table keys. That is the subject of the next chapter.

</details>
