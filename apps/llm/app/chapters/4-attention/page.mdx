# Attention

*How a token reads the rest of the sentence. The one mechanism that turned a
pile of word vectors into something that understands language.*

Read this sentence: *The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too
big.* What does *it* point to — the trophy, or the suitcase? You answered
instantly, and you answered *trophy*. Now read this one: *The trophy didn't fit
in the suitcase because it was too small.* One word changed, and *it* flipped to
*suitcase*.

To resolve that pronoun you did something specific: you let the word *it* reach
back across the sentence and pull in the words that give it meaning. You didn't
read left to right and forget; you let later words *look at* earlier ones. That
looking-back is the whole idea of this chapter. It is called **attention**, and
it is the mechanism that the entire transformer — and every large language model
built on one — is organised around.

By the end of this chapter you will know exactly what a token "looking at"
another token means, down to the arithmetic, and you will have watched a real
GPT-2 do it. You will have implemented attention from scratch in a dozen lines of
NumPy. And the word *it* will have reached across a sentence, in front of you, and
grabbed the word it needed.

## 1. The problem attention solves

In the last chapter each token became a vector — a point in space that captures
something about the word's meaning. But a word's meaning is not fixed. *Bank*
near *river* is a slope of earth; *bank* near *money* is a building. The vector
for *bank* coming out of the embedding table is the same in both sentences. To be
useful, a token's representation has to *absorb its context* — it has to become a
blend of itself and the words around it.

So the job is this: take a sequence of token vectors, and produce a new sequence
where each vector has been enriched by the others. The naive version is easy —
just average every token with all the others. But a flat average is hopeless: it
treats *the* and *trophy* as equally relevant to *it*, when only one of them
matters. What we want is a **weighted** average, where each token decides, for
itself, how much to draw from every other token. The weights should be different
for every token, and they should depend on the actual content.

That is attention: a learned, content-dependent, weighted average. Everything
that follows is just *how* those weights get computed.

## 2. Soft lookup: query, key, value

Here is the mechanism, in plain terms first.

Every token produces three vectors. A **query** — what am I looking for? A
**key** — what do I have to offer? And a **value** — what will I actually hand
over if you pick me? The names come from databases, and the analogy is exact: a
query is matched against keys, and the matching entries return their values.

A token's query is compared against *every* token's key by taking a dot product.
A large dot product means the query and key point the same way — a strong match.
Those match scores are pushed through a **softmax**, which turns them into
positive weights that sum to one. Finally, the token's new representation is the
sum of all the values, weighted by those scores. A token has now read the whole
sentence and kept a custom-weighted blend of it.

Crucially, the weights are *soft*. The token doesn't pick one other token; it
spreads its attention, mostly onto a few and a little onto the rest. *it* can
pour 60% of its attention onto *suitcase*, 20% onto *trophy*, and dribble the
rest across the sentence. That softness is what makes attention differentiable —
and so trainable by the gradient descent of the previous book.

Sit with the default view for a moment. We have opened on layer 9, head 0, with

land squarely on *suitcase*. This single head, somewhere in the middle of the
network, has learned to do coreference. Nobody told it to. It fell out of
predicting the next token across billions of words of text.

Now explore. Slide to the early layers and the picture changes entirely: tokens
attend mostly to their immediate neighbours, or pile onto the very first token (a
kind of resting position heads adopt when they have nothing to say). Different
heads in the same layer attend to wildly different things — position, syntax,
repetition. That a single model holds 144 of these heads, each a small specialist,
is the subject of the next chapter.

## 4. The same thing, as an equation

Now that you have felt it, here is the formula. Stack the per-token queries into a
matrix $Q$, the keys into $K$, the values into $V$. Then

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.$$

Read it against what you just watched. $QK^\top$ is every query dotted with every
key — the raw match scores, one for each cell in the grid above. The softmax,
applied along each row, turns each token's scores into the weights you saw summing
to one. Multiplying by $V$ takes the weighted average of the value vectors. That's
the entire operation.

The only piece without an obvious counterpart is the $\sqrt{d_k}$. Dot products
grow with dimension, and large scores make the softmax peaky — almost one-hot —
which starves the gradient. Dividing by the square root of the key dimension keeps
the scores in a sane range. It is a scaling convenience, not a deep idea, but
leave it out and training stalls.

## 5. Looking only backwards

Notice what the grid above never shows: anything above the diagonal. Token 3 can
attend to tokens 1, 2, and 3, but never to token 4. This is the **causal mask**,
and it exists because the model's only job is to predict the *next* token. If a
token could see the future, predicting it would be cheating — the answer would
leak straight into the input.

Mechanically, the mask is brutal and simple: before the softmax, set every score
where $j > i$ to $-\infty$. The softmax sends those to exactly zero, and each
token is sealed off from everything that comes after it. The lower-triangular
shape you see in every map is the visible fingerprint of that rule.

## 6. Implementing it yourself

Attention is far simpler than its reputation. Here is the whole thing — scaled
dot-product attention with a causal mask — in NumPy. Run it and read the printed
weight matrix: each row sums to one, and the upper triangle is zero, exactly like
the maps above.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def attention(Q, K, V, causal=True):
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)          # every query dotted with every key
    if causal:
        n = scores.shape[0]
        mask = np.triu(np.ones((n, n)), k=1).astype(bool)
        scores[mask] = -np.inf               # no looking at the future
    weights = softmax(scores, axis=-1)       # rows sum to 1
    return weights @ V, weights

# Five tokens, each a small random query/key/value vector.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d = 5, 8
Q, K, V = rng.standard_normal((n, d)), rng.standard_normal((n, d)), rng.standard_normal((n, d))

out, weights = attention(Q, K, V)
print("attention weights (rows sum to 1, upper triangle zero):")
print(np.round(weights, 2))
print("\\nrow sums:", np.round(weights.sum(axis=1), 3))
print("output shape:", out.shape)
`}
/>

That function, run over and over with learned projections producing $Q$, $K$, and
$V$, is the beating heart of a transformer. The rest of the architecture — which
we build over the next chapters — is scaffolding around this one weighted average.

Early layers are the easiest place to look. At layer 0, most heads have *it*

and surface form, later layers handle meaning and reference.

### Problem 2 — Temperature in disguise

The $\sqrt{d_k}$ divisor controls how peaky the attention weights are. Modify the
implementation: multiply the scores by a factor before the softmax and watch the
weights. What happens as the factor grows large? As it shrinks toward zero?

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
scores = rng.standard_normal(6)

for factor in [0.1, 1.0, 10.0]:
    w = softmax(scores * factor)
    print(f"factor {factor:>4}:  {np.round(w, 3)}")

`}

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

Large factors make the softmax approach a hard *argmax*: nearly all the weight
collapses onto the single highest-scoring token (one-hot). Small factors flatten
it toward a uniform average over all tokens. The factor is exactly an inverse
temperature — the same dial we'll meet again in Chapter 14 when we sample the
model's output distribution. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ is just choosing a sensible
default temperature so that, at initialisation, attention is neither uniform mush
nor a brittle one-hot.

</details>
