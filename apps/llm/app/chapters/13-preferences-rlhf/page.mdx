# Learning from preferences

*For most of what we ask, there is no single correct answer — only better and worse
ones. The last training stage learns that distinction from human comparisons.*

Instruction-tuning (Chapter 12) taught the model to answer by *imitating* good
responses. But imitation has a ceiling: it can only reproduce the demonstrations it
was shown, and for open-ended requests — *write me a poem*, *explain this gently*,
*is this safe?* — there is no one demonstration to copy. There are countless
acceptable answers, varying in helpfulness, honesty, tone, and safety, and what we
actually want is for the model to produce *better* ones.

The breakthrough idea is to stop trying to specify the perfect answer and instead
learn from **comparisons**. People are bad at writing the ideal response from
scratch but very good at looking at two responses and saying which is better. Collect
enough of those judgments and you can train the model toward what people prefer.

By the end of this chapter you will have trained a reward model by clicking, and
implemented the preference loss that underlies RLHF and DPO.

## 1. From imitation to preference

The shift is from "here is the right answer, copy it" to "here are two answers, this
one is better." That second signal is far easier to collect at scale and far more
expressive — it can capture *be more concise*, *don't make things up*, *refuse
this* — preferences no one could fully enumerate as demonstrations. Try it: pick the
better response a few times and watch a reward model learn your preference.

<PreferenceTuning caption="Figure 13.1 — Training a reward model from comparisons. Each click is one human preference; the model updates (Bradley-Terry) toward the response you chose, and its predicted preference shifts. Real systems gather millions of such comparisons." />

Each click is a single human judgment. The reward model's job is to *generalise*
from many such judgments — to assign a scalar "reward" to any response such that
preferred responses score higher. Once you have that, you have turned a fuzzy human
value into a number the model can be optimised against.

## 2. The reward model

Concretely: collect a dataset of (prompt, response A, response B, *which was
preferred*). Train a model — usually a copy of the language model with a scalar
output head — to predict a reward $r$ for any (prompt, response) such that the
preferred response gets the higher reward. The training objective says simply: the
probability assigned to the human's choice should be high, where that probability is
a logistic function of the reward *difference*. This is the **Bradley-Terry** model,
the same math behind chess Elo.

## 3. Optimising the policy: RLHF and DPO

With a reward model in hand, you tune the language model (the **policy**) to produce
responses the reward model scores highly — while staying close to the instruction-
tuned starting point, so it doesn't wander off into gibberish that happens to fool
the reward. The classic recipe, **RLHF**, does this with reinforcement learning
(PPO): generate, score, update. It works but is fiddly.

A newer method, **DPO** (direct preference optimisation), skips the separate reward
model and RL loop entirely: it rearranges the math so you can train the policy
*directly* on the preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss. Same
goal — move probability toward preferred responses, away from dispreferred ones —
far less machinery. Much of the field has moved to DPO and its relatives.

## 4. What it buys, and what it costs

Preference training is the stage most responsible for the *character* of a modern
assistant — its helpfulness, its honesty, its refusals. It is also where the
sharpest failure modes live. Optimise too hard against an imperfect reward model and
you get **reward hacking** — responses that score well but are worse (verbose,
sycophantic, confidently wrong). Push safety too far and you get **over-refusal**.
And the model inherits the values of whoever produced the comparisons. Alignment is
not solved by this stage; it is *implemented* by it, with all the difficulty that
implies.

## 5. The preference loss

The heart of it is one line. Given the reward model's scores for the chosen and
rejected responses, the loss makes the chosen one's reward higher.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def sigmoid(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

# Reward model's scores for the human-chosen and human-rejected responses.
# Bradley-Terry loss: -log P(chosen preferred) = -log sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected).
def preference_loss(r_chosen, r_rejected):
    return -np.log(sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected))

# Before training: the model wrongly prefers the rejected answer.
r_chosen, r_rejected = 0.2, 0.9
print(f"loss before: {preference_loss(r_chosen, r_rejected):.3f}")

# One gradient step on this pair (push chosen up, rejected down).
lr = 0.5
for step in range(8):
    p = sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected)
    g = (1 - p) * lr
    r_chosen += g; r_rejected -= g
    print(f"step {step}: r_chosen={r_chosen:+.2f}  r_rejected={r_rejected:+.2f}  "
          f"loss={preference_loss(r_chosen, r_rejected):.3f}")
`}
/>

That single comparison loss, summed over millions of human judgments and used to
shape the policy, is what stands between a raw instruction-follower and an assistant
people actually want to talk to.

## 6. Problems

### Problem 1 — Why comparisons, not scores?

Annotators are asked *"which response is better, A or B?"* rather than *"rate this
response 0–10."* Why are pairwise comparisons easier to collect reliably, and how
does the §5 loss use them?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

Absolute scores are noisy and inconsistent across people and over time — one
annotator's 7 is another's 4, and the scale drifts. A pairwise "A is better than B"
is a far more stable judgment. The Bradley-Terry loss needs only that ordering: it
maximises the probability of the chosen response under a logistic function of the
reward *difference*, so only relative reward matters, never an absolute scale. Easier
to label, and exactly what the loss consumes.

</details>

### Problem 2 — Reward hacking

Suppose the reward model has learned that longer answers tend to be preferred.
Optimise the policy hard against it and what degenerate behaviour might emerge? What
does this say about treating the reward model's score as the true objective?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

The policy will learn to pad — produce needlessly long, waffly answers that the
reward model scores highly but humans actually find worse. This is reward hacking:
the reward model is a *proxy* for human preference, and optimising a proxy too hard
exploits its gaps (Goodhart's law). It's why RLHF keeps the policy close to its
starting point and why reward models must be monitored and refreshed — the score is
a useful signal, not the real goal.

</details>
