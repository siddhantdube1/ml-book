# Scaling laws

*The single most consequential empirical fact about language models: make them
bigger and feed them more, and the loss falls — smoothly, predictably, for orders
of magnitude.*

You might expect that improving a model is a craft — clever architectures, lucky
tricks, diminishing and erratic returns. For language models, the dominant factor
turned out to be embarrassingly blunt: **scale**. Across an enormous range, test
loss falls as a smooth **power law** in three quantities — the amount of compute,
the number of parameters, and the number of training tokens. Not noisily; on a
log-log plot, a near-straight line.

This predictability is why the last several years happened the way they did. If you
can measure the loss of small models and *extrapolate* the curve, you can justify
spending a hundred million dollars on a big one before you train it — because you
already know, roughly, what loss you'll get.

By the end of this chapter you will have explored the curve, met compute-optimal
training, and fit a scaling law yourself.

## 1. The curve

Here is the shape. Loss as a function of training compute, falling toward an
**irreducible floor** — the entropy of language itself, which no amount of compute
can beat.

<ScalingLaws caption="Figure 11.1 — Illustrative scaling curve, shaped like the empirical results of Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. Loss falls as a power of compute toward a floor; the readouts show the compute-optimal model size and token count for each budget." />

The defining feature is *predictability*. The relationship is well described by

$$L(C) \approx E + \left(\frac{C_0}{C}\right)^{\alpha},$$

where $E$ is the irreducible loss, $C$ the compute, and $\alpha$ a small exponent.
Because the exponent is small, gains slow down — each further drop in loss costs
roughly an order of magnitude more compute — but they do not stop, and crucially
they do not surprise. Slide the budget and watch the loss track the curve.

## 2. Three knobs, one law

The compute you spend is, to a good approximation, $C \approx 6ND$ — six times the
number of parameters $N$ times the number of training tokens $D$. Loss follows a
power law in each: bigger model, more data, more compute, lower loss. The three are
linked, so the real question is not "how big a model?" but "given a fixed compute
budget, how should I split it between model size and data?"

## 3. Compute-optimal training

The famous answer — from the *Chinchilla* paper — is that for a fixed compute
budget there is an **optimal balance**: scale parameters and tokens *together*,
roughly in proportion, at about 20 training tokens per parameter. The headline
consequence was that many large models of the era were badly **undertrained** —
too big for the data they saw. A smaller model trained on more tokens beat a larger
one trained on fewer, at the same cost. The readouts under Figure 11.1 show this
optimal split: as you slide the compute budget, the optimal parameter count and
token count both rise together.

## 4. Why it matters, and where it bends

Scaling laws turned model-building into something closer to engineering: measure
small, extrapolate, provision compute, predict the result. But they are empirical,
not eternal. They assume abundant fresh data — and the highest-quality text is
finite, a ceiling the field is now pressing against. And loss is not the same as
usefulness: a smooth drop in next-token loss can produce *jumps* in apparent
capability, which is its own active area of study. The curve is a remarkably
reliable guide, right up until the regime where its assumptions stop holding.

## 5. Fitting a scaling law

A power law is a straight line in log-log space, so you fit one with ordinary linear
regression on the logs.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

# Loss measured for a few small training runs (compute in FLOPs).
C = np.array([1e18, 1e19, 1e20, 1e21])
L = np.array([3.40, 3.05, 2.74, 2.47])

# Assume L - E follows a power law in C; fit in log-log space.
E = 1.5                          # assume the irreducible floor
y = np.log(L - E)
x = np.log(C)
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
alpha = -slope
print(f"fitted exponent alpha = {alpha:.3f}")

# Extrapolate: predict the loss at 1000x the largest run.
C_new = 1e24
L_pred = E + np.exp(intercept) * C_new ** slope
print(f"predicted loss at C=1e24: {L_pred:.3f}")
print("(measured the curve on cheap runs, predicted an expensive one)")
`}
/>

That extrapolation — fit cheaply, predict expensively — is the entire business case
for scale. You are reading the future loss of a model you have not yet trained.

## 6. Problems

### Problem 1 — Spend a budget

Using Figure 11.1, pick a compute budget and read off the optimal parameters and
tokens. Now imagine instead building a model twice as large at the same budget.
Using the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule, what must give, and what does Chinchilla
predict about the result?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

At a fixed compute budget ($C \approx 6ND$), doubling the parameters $N$ forces the
token count $D$ to roughly halve. That pushes you well below the ~20-tokens-per-
parameter optimum into the *undertrained* regime, and Chinchilla predicts a *worse*
final loss than the compute-optimal split — you'd have done better with a smaller
model trained on more data. Bigger is not free; it must be paid for in data.

</details>

### Problem 2 — Diminishing but not stopping

The exponent $\alpha$ in §5 is small (well under 1). Using the fitted law, how much
more compute does it take to halve the *reducible* loss $L - E$? What does that imply
about the cost of each additional increment of quality?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

If $L - E \propto C^{-\alpha}$ with small $\alpha$, halving $L - E$ requires
multiplying compute by $2^{1/\alpha}$ — and for $\alpha \approx 0.05$ that is
$2^{20}$, about a million-fold. Each further increment of quality costs
exponentially more compute. Returns diminish sharply, which is why progress leans on
ever-larger compute budgets and on changing the game (better data, new objectives)
rather than riding a single curve forever.

</details>
