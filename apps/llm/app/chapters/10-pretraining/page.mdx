# Pretraining

*The architecture is an empty machine. Pretraining is the long process that fills
it — by playing the next-token game across much of what humanity has written.*

We have built a transformer that turns a context into a next-token distribution
(Part II), but with random weights it predicts nonsense. Everything a model knows —
grammar, facts, the rudiments of reasoning — is installed in one enormous training
phase called **pretraining**: show the model staggering amounts of text and, at
every position, nudge its weights to better predict the token that actually came
next.

That is the whole loop. There is no separate "teach it facts" step. The model
learns the capital of France because the string *the capital of France is Paris*
appears in its training data, and predicting *Paris* well requires storing that
fact. Competence is a by-product of relentless next-token prediction.

By the end of this chapter you will have seen the literal training signal on a real
model and computed the objective that pretraining minimises.

## 1. Self-supervision: labels for free

The reason this scales to trillions of examples is that it needs **no human
labels**. For supervised learning you normally pay people to annotate data; here
the data annotates itself. Take any sentence, and for every position the "correct
answer" is simply the next token already sitting there. A document of 1,000 tokens
is 1,000 training examples, free. Point the model at a large fraction of the web,
books, and code, and you have more supervision than any labelling budget could buy.
This is **self-supervised** learning, and it is the trick that made scale possible.

## 2. The training signal, made visible

What does the model actually get corrected on? At each position it predicts a
distribution; we look at the probability it gave to the *real* next token, and the
loss is how *surprised* it was — low if it expected the token, high if it didn't.
Here is that surprisal on a real GPT-2, token by token:

Read the bright spots: they are exactly where the model was *wrong* or unsure, and
so exactly where training pushes hardest. Predictable continuations are already
dim — the model loses little there. Pretraining, billions of times over, is nothing
but the gradual dimming of this picture across all of language. The average
surprisal has a name — it is the **cross-entropy loss**, and two-to-its-power is
**perplexity**, the headline number for how well a model predicts text.

## 3. The data, and why it matters

The other half of pretraining is the corpus. Modern models train on trillions of
tokens scraped from the web, books, and code, then aggressively filtered and
deduplicated — because the model learns *whatever the data contains*, including its
mistakes and biases. Quality and diversity of data turn out to matter as much as
raw quantity: a model is a compression of its training set, and you cannot compress
your way to knowledge that was never in there.

## 4. What pretraining actually installs

Because predicting text *well* requires modelling whatever produced the text
(Chapter 1), the pressure to lower that loss installs a hierarchy of competence —
first spelling and grammar, then facts and associations, then fragments of
reasoning — all as side effects of compression. None of it is targeted. It emerges
because each capability happens to lower next-token loss on the kind of text where
it's needed. How far this goes as you spend more is the subject of the next chapter.

## 5. Computing the objective

Here is the loss pretraining minimises — cross-entropy and perplexity — over a
handful of positions, from scratch.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

# Suppose at four positions the model gave these probabilities to the
# token that actually came next:
p_true = np.array([0.62, 0.09, 0.95, 0.30])

# Surprisal (bits) of each token, and the average = cross-entropy.
surprisal = -np.log2(p_true)
cross_entropy = surprisal.mean()
perplexity = 2 ** cross_entropy

for p, s in zip(p_true, surprisal):
    print(f"p={p:.2f}  ->  {s:.2f} bits of surprise")
print(f"\\ncross-entropy = {cross_entropy:.3f} bits/token")
print(f"perplexity    = {perplexity:.2f}  (effective branching factor)")

# Training lowers this: pretend the model improves on the worst token.
p_better = p_true.copy(); p_better[1] = 0.40
print("\\nafter learning the hard token:",
      f"perplexity {2 ** (-np.log2(p_better)).mean():.2f}")
`}
/>

Lowering that one number, averaged over a corpus the size of the internet, is the
entire goal of pretraining. Everything the model can do is a means to that end.

## 6. Problems

### Problem 1 — Read the signal

In Figure 10.1, switch between sentences and find the single most surprising token
and the least surprising. Why is the model so unsurprised by some tokens, and what
does it mean that early words in a sentence tend to be surprising?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

The least surprising tokens are highly constrained by context — *Paris* after *the
capital of France is*, or *oxygen* after *hydrogen and* — where almost only one
word fits. The most surprising tend to be content words with little preceding
context, especially early in the sentence, where the model has little to condition
on. High surprisal marks exactly the positions where there's something to learn;
predictable positions contribute almost nothing to the loss.

</details>

### Problem 2 — Perplexity intuition

A model with perplexity 20 is, loosely, "as confused as if choosing uniformly among
20 words" at each step. Using §5, what perplexity does a model achieve if it always
puts probability 1.0 on the correct token? What about a uniform guess over a 50,000-
token vocabulary?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

Perfect prediction (p = 1.0 everywhere) gives surprisal 0 and perplexity
2⁰ = **1** — no confusion at all. A uniform guess over 50,000 tokens gives
surprisal log₂(50000) ≈ 15.6 bits and perplexity ≈ **50,000** — maximal confusion.
Real models sit far below the uniform baseline (perplexities in the low tens or
single digits on typical text), and pretraining is the process of pushing from the
50,000 end toward the 1 end.

</details>
