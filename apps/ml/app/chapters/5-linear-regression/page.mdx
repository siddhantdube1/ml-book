# Linear regression

*The mother of all models. The line of best fit — a loss that says what "best"
means, and a formula that finds it — and the question whose answer is the rest of
the book.*

Plot a scatter of points that trend upward — hours studied against exam score,
square footage against house price, advertising spend against sales — and your eye
draws a line through them almost involuntarily. Linear regression is that instinct
made precise. It is the oldest model in statistics, the simplest model that
genuinely *learns* by fitting parameters to data, and the conceptual ancestor of an
astonishing amount of what follows — logistic regression, neural networks, and more
are all, at heart, linear regression with extra steps. If you understand this
chapter deeply, much of the rest of the book is variations on it.

Unlike the k-nearest neighbours of Chapter 4, which kept the whole dataset and
consulted it at prediction time, linear regression *distils* the data into a handful
of numbers — a slope and an intercept — and then throws the data away. That is the
shape of nearly every model to come: a small set of learned parameters that capture
the pattern. To find them we need to say exactly what "best fit" means, which means a
loss; and then we need to minimise that loss, which for this one model we can do with
a clean formula — and the moment we ask what happens when we *can't*, we have walked
straight into Chapter 6.

## 1. Fitting a line to a trend

The setup: you have pairs of numbers — an input $x$ and an output $y$ for each
example — and you believe $y$ rises (or falls) roughly linearly with $x$. You want a
rule that predicts $y$ from $x$, both to understand the relationship and to predict
$y$ for new inputs you have not measured. A straight line is the simplest such rule,
and often a remarkably good one.

The art is that infinitely many lines could be drawn through a cloud of points, and
they are not equally good. Some pass right through the heart of the trend; others
sit too high, too low, or at the wrong tilt. We need a principled way to pick the
single best line — not by eye, but by a definition we can compute and optimise. That
definition is the heart of the chapter.

## 2. The model

A line is described by two numbers, so the model has exactly two parameters:

$$\hat y = w x + b.$$

Here $w$ is the **slope** (how much $\hat y$ rises per unit of $x$) and $b$ is the
**intercept** (the value of $\hat y$ when $x = 0$); the hat on $\hat y$ marks it as a
*prediction*, not the true observed value. Choosing the model means committing to
this straight-line shape; fitting it means finding the particular $w$ and $b$ that
best match the data.

<LineFit />

Drag the handles to tilt and shift the line. Every position is a different choice of
$(w, b)$ — a different model — and most of them fit the points badly. The question
is which one fits *best*, and to answer it we must first measure how badly any given
line fits, point by point.

## 3. Residuals and the loss

For each data point, the line makes a prediction $\hat y = w x + b$, and the true
value $y$ usually differs from it. That gap, $y - \hat y$, is the **residual** — the
miss, the orange stub in the widget above. A good line makes the residuals small
across all the points; a bad line leaves big ones.

To turn "small residuals" into a single number to minimise, we **square** each
residual and average them — the **mean squared error** (MSE):

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - (w x_i + b)\big)^2.$$

Squaring does two jobs: it makes every residual positive (so misses above and below
the line both count, and cannot cancel out), and it punishes big misses far more than
small ones (a residual of 2 contributes four times as much as a residual of 1), which
makes the line lean toward avoiding large errors. This MSE is the **loss** — the
scorekeeper from Chapter 1 — and fitting the model means finding the $(w, b)$ that
make it as small as possible.

## 4. Least squares

So the goal is precise: among all lines, find the one that minimises the mean squared
error. This is the **least-squares** criterion, and it has a beautiful geometric
reading.

<LeastSquares />

Draw each residual as an actual square — side equal to the size of the miss, so its
*area* is the residual squared — and the total orange area is the sum of squared
errors, the loss itself. Tilt the line and the squares swell and shrink. The best-fit
line is, quite literally, the one that makes the **total area of the squares as small
as it can be**. That is the entire meaning of "least squares": least total squared
area. And because the loss is a smooth, bowl-shaped function of $w$ and $b$ — squaring
makes it a clean parabola in each parameter — it has exactly one minimum, with no
local traps to worry about.

## 5. The closed form: the normal equations

Here is what makes linear regression special, even among the models in this book: its
minimum can be found by a *formula*, with no iteration at all. Because the loss is
quadratic in the parameters, its slope with respect to $w$ and $b$ is linear, and
setting those slopes to zero — the flat-tangent condition for a minimum from Chapter 3 —
gives a system of linear equations, the **normal equations**, that we can solve
directly.

Writing the data as a matrix $X$ (one row per example, a column of 1s for the
intercept) and the targets as a vector $y$, the least-squares solution is

$$w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y.$$

You do not need to memorise this, only to register what it means: **the best line is
available in closed form** — one matrix computation, exact, no guessing, no steps.
This is a rare luxury. It exists because the model is linear and the loss is squared
error, a combination so clean it solves itself. Hold onto how special that is,
because §9 is about everything losing it.

## 6. Multiple features

Nothing confines linear regression to a single input. With several features —
predicting a house price from its area, age, *and* number of bedrooms — the model
becomes

$$\hat y = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_d x_d + b = w \cdot x + b,$$

the weighted sum, the dot product of Chapter 3, with one weight per feature. With two
features the "line" becomes a *plane* tilted through three-dimensional space; with
more, a flat **hyperplane** in a space we cannot picture but the algebra handles
without complaint. Remarkably, the same closed-form normal equations solve it — the
formula $w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$ does not care how many columns $X$ has. One
matrix solve fits a model in any number of dimensions.

## 7. How good is the fit?

A fitted line always exists, but is it any good? The standard score is **R²**, the
*coefficient of determination*: the fraction of the variation in $y$ that the model
explains.

<ResidualsR2 />

R² runs from 1 (a perfect fit — the line passes through every point) down to 0 (the
line is no better than just guessing the mean of $y$ every time), and it can even go
negative for a truly terrible fit. Turn up the noise and watch it fall: the same
straight-line model explains less and less as the data scatters further from any line,
because more of the variation is simply random and unexplainable. The residual plot on
the right is the other half of the diagnosis — for a good linear fit it should look
like *structureless noise* scattered around zero. If you see a pattern there — a curve,
a fan, a trend — the straight line has missed something systematic, and a different
model is called for.

## 8. Reading the coefficients

Beyond prediction, linear regression is prized because its parameters are
*interpretable*. The weight $w_j$ on a feature is the predicted change in $y$ for a
one-unit increase in that feature, *holding the others fixed* — "each extra bedroom
adds about £15,000, all else equal". This transparency is a real strength; few models
let you read off the influence of each input so directly.

But two cautions, both important. First, the units matter: a weight looks small or
large depending on the feature's scale, so to compare importances you must standardise
the features first (Chapter 17). Second, and graver: **a coefficient is an
association, not a cause.** That ice-cream sales predict drownings does not mean ice
cream drowns people — both are driven by hot weather. Regression finds what moves
*with* what; it cannot, on its own, tell you what moves *because* of what. Reading
causation into regression coefficients is one of the most common and costly mistakes
in all of data analysis.

## 9. When the formula runs out

We have a model fit by an exact formula — so why is there a whole book ahead? Because
the closed form is a happy accident of *this specific combination*: a linear model and
a squared-error loss. Change either and the formula evaporates. Swap the loss (to
classify, Chapter 7 needs a different one), or swap the model (for the curves of trees
and the layers of neural networks), and there is no neat equation that hands you the
minimum. Even for linear regression with millions of features, computing $(X^\top
X)^{-1}$ becomes too expensive to do directly.

So for almost everything else in this book, we cannot solve for the best parameters;
we have to **search** for them — start somewhere, measure which way is downhill, take
a step, and repeat, sliding down the loss surface toward its minimum. That search is
**gradient descent**, the single most important algorithm in machine learning, and it
is the subject of the very next chapter. Linear regression is where the loss-minimising
view of learning is born; gradient descent is how it is carried to every model that
has no formula of its own.

## 10. Implement it yourself

The closed-form least-squares fit — the normal equations — in a few lines, and a check
that it matches a built-in solver.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.linspace(0, 10, 30)
y = 0.8 * x + 1.8 + rng.normal(0, 0.9, 30)      # a line, plus noise

# Build the design matrix X: a column of 1s (for the intercept) and a column of x.
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])

# Normal equations: w = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y  — the exact least-squares solution.
w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
print(f"intercept b = {w[0]:.3f},  slope w = {w[1]:.3f}")

# It matches numpy's built-in fit exactly:
print("polyfit gives:", np.polyfit(x, y, 1).round(3), "(slope, intercept)")

# R^2: the fraction of variance explained.
yhat = X @ w
r2 = 1 - np.sum((y - yhat) ** 2) / np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
print(f"R^2 = {r2:.3f}")
`}
/>

One linear solve gives the exact best-fit line — no iteration, no learning rate, no
guessing — because the squared-error loss on a linear model has a closed-form minimum.
Savour it: linear regression is the last model in this book that solves itself. From
Chapter 6 onward, we descend.

## 11. Problems

### Problem 1 — Residuals, squares, and R²

A conceptual question. (a) Why does the loss square the residuals rather than just
adding their absolute values? (b) What does an R² of 0 mean, and what does an R² of 1
mean? (c) After fitting, you plot the residuals against $x$ and see a clear U-shaped
pattern. What does that tell you, and what would you do?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** Squaring makes every residual positive, so misses above and below the line
both count and cannot cancel out; and it penalises large misses disproportionately (a
miss of 2 counts four times a miss of 1), pulling the line toward avoiding big errors.
Crucially, squaring also makes the loss a smooth quadratic, which is what gives it a
single minimum solvable in closed form — absolute value has a kink that does not.

**(b)** R² = 1 means a perfect fit: the line passes exactly through every point and
explains all the variation in $y$. R² = 0 means the line explains none of it — it does
no better than ignoring $x$ and always predicting the mean of $y$.

**(c)** A systematic pattern in the residuals means the straight line has missed real
structure — here, a U-shape says the true relationship curves, and the line is
under- then over- then under-predicting. The fix is a more flexible model: add a
quadratic term (a polynomial feature, Chapter 17) or use a model that can bend. Good
residuals look like patternless noise.

</details>

y = 1.3 * x - 2.0 + rng.normal(0, 1.2, 40)

X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])

y$.

The capstone caution. Fit a line to data that genuinely curves, and read the warning

#   3. then fit a degree-2 polynomial and compare R^2

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 50)
y = x**2 + rng.normal(0, 1.0, 50)

def r2(p):
    yhat = np.polyval(p, x)
    return 1 - np.sum((y - yhat)**2) / np.sum((y - y.mean())**2)

p1 = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
p2 = np.polyfit(x, y, 2)
print(f"line  R^2 = {r2(p1):.3f}")
print(f"quadratic R^2 = {r2(p2):.3f}")

resid = y - np.polyval(p1, x)
print("line residuals (left/middle/right means):",
      round(resid[:16].mean(), 1), round(resid[16:34].mean(), 1), round(resid[34:].mean(), 1))
`}
/>

The straight line has a dismal R² and residuals that are positive at the ends and
negative in the middle — a clear U-shaped pattern betraying that the truth curves. The
degree-2 fit, which can bend, has a far higher R² and patternless residuals. Linear
regression is only as good as the assumption that the relationship *is* linear; when it
is not, the residuals will tell you, and a more flexible model — built from the very
features and methods of the rest of this book — is the answer.

</details>

You have now seen a model fit by minimising a loss, and met the one case where that
minimisation has a clean, closed-form answer. Almost nothing else in machine learning
is so obliging. The line of best fit is where the entire loss-minimising view of
learning begins — and the question it leaves open, *what do we do when there is no
formula?*, is the doorway to everything that follows.
