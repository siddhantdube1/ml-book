# The ML workflow

*How to actually build a model and trust it — by holding data back, watching for
overfitting, and never, ever fooling yourself.*

A student who has seen last year's exam can ace it without understanding a thing —
they have simply memorised the answers. Hand them this year's exam, with different
questions, and the bluff collapses. The only honest test of understanding is
performance on questions they have *not* seen before. Machine learning has exactly
this problem, and exactly this solution. A model that has memorised its training
data will report glowing numbers on that data and fail the moment it meets anything
new — so we must always set aside a fresh "exam" the model never studied, and judge
it only on that.

This is the heart of the machine-learning *workflow*: the disciplined process of
turning data into a model you can actually rely on. It is less glamorous than the
algorithms, and far more important. A mediocre model evaluated honestly is worth
more than a brilliant one evaluated by self-deception, because only the first tells
you the truth about how it will behave in the world. Most failures of applied
machine learning are not failures of the model; they are failures of the workflow —
someone fooled themselves.

By the end of this chapter you will know how to split your data so the evaluation
is honest, how to recognise the twin failures of underfitting and overfitting, the
bias–variance trade-off that underlies them, and the iron rule that protects you
from self-deception. These ideas govern every model in the rest of the book.

## 1. The goal, restated sharply

Chapter 1 said the goal is to *generalise* — to do well on unseen data. The
workflow is how we hold ourselves to that goal instead of quietly drifting toward
the easier, useless one of doing well on the data we already have.

The danger is subtle because it does not feel like cheating. You fit a model, you
measure its error on your data, the error is low, you are pleased. But that number
answers the wrong question. It tells you how well the model fits the examples it was
trained on — which, for a flexible enough model, can always be made perfect and
means nothing. The question you actually care about is how well it will predict
*tomorrow's* data, which by definition you do not have yet. The entire workflow is a
trick for estimating that future performance honestly, today.

## 2. The train/test split

The trick is simple: before you do anything else, take your data and **split it in
two**. The larger part, the **training set** (typically 70–80%), is what the model
learns from. The smaller part, the **test set** (the remaining 20–30%), you lock in
a drawer and do not look at. You fit the model on the training set alone, and then —
once, at the very end — you measure its error on the test set.

Because the model never saw the test points during fitting, its error on them is a
fair stand-in for its error on genuinely new data. The test set is a simulated
future: data from the same source that the model has not been allowed to study. The
split must be done *first*, before any fitting or even any inspection, and the test
set must stay untouched, or the simulation is contaminated and the estimate lies.

## 3. Fitting on the training set

With the split made, fitting proceeds exactly as Chapter 1 described — a model, a
loss, an optimiser — but using *only* the training data. The model tunes its
parameters to make its predictions match the training examples, driving the
training loss down. Nothing about the test set enters this process; the test points
do not influence a single parameter. They are waiting in the drawer.

This separation is what makes the test error meaningful. If the test data had leaked
into training — even indirectly, even by your having peeked at it to choose a model —
then the model would have an unfair advantage on it, and its test error would
flatter it. The whole value of the held-out set is that it is *held out*.

## 4. The test set is the future

Now collect the model's verdict.

<TrainTestSplit />

The filled points trained the model; the hollow points were held back, and the
model is judged on how far its curve lands from them — its **test error**. When the
test error is close to the training error, as it is here, the model has genuinely
captured the trend and you can expect it to behave on new data much as it does on
this test set. When the test error is far worse than the training error, something
has gone wrong — and the most common something is overfitting.

## 5. Underfitting and overfitting

There are two ways to fail, and they pull in opposite directions. Drag the model's
complexity through its range and watch both appear.

<OverfitDegree />

**Underfitting** is failure by simplicity: the model is too rigid to capture the
real pattern, so it does poorly on *both* the training and the test data. The fix
is more flexibility. **Overfitting** is failure by complexity: the model is so
flexible that it fits not only the underlying pattern but the random noise in the
training points, threading through every one of them. Its training error drops
toward zero — it looks brilliant on the data it studied — but it has learned
spurious wiggles that do not generalise, so its test error climbs. The fix is less
flexibility (or more data, or regularisation, Chapter 9). The art is finding the
balance: complex enough to capture the signal, simple enough to ignore the noise.

## 6. The complexity U

Plot the two errors against model complexity and the whole story compresses into one
picture — arguably the most important figure in the book.

<ComplexityCurve />

Training error falls monotonically: more complexity can only fit the training data
better, all the way to zero. Test error traces a **U** — high on the left
(underfitting), falling to a minimum, then rising on the right (overfitting). The
bottom of that U is the model you want, and its location is a property of your data
and your model that you discover, not decree. Every act of model selection — choosing
a polynomial degree, a tree depth, the number of hidden units — is a search for the
bottom of this curve, guided always by held-out error and never by training error,
which would happily march you off the overfitting cliff.

## 7. Bias and variance, plainly

The two failures have names that recur throughout the book. **Bias** is error from
wrong assumptions — a model too simple to represent the truth, like a straight line
forced onto a curve. High bias is underfitting. **Variance** is error from
sensitivity to the particular training sample — a model so flexible that a different
draw of training points would give a wildly different fit. High variance is
overfitting.

The two trade off. Simple models have high bias and low variance: they miss the
pattern but are stable. Complex models have low bias and high variance: they can
capture anything but swing around with the noise. Total error is, loosely, bias plus
variance, and the U-curve is that sum — bias dominating on the left, variance on the
right, their balance at the bottom. The **bias–variance trade-off** is just the
complexity U, seen through the lens of *why* each side fails. Reducing one usually
raises the other; the goal is the sweet spot that minimises their sum.

## 8. The validation set

There is a catch in §6 we glossed over. If you use the *test* set to choose the best
complexity — fitting many models and keeping the one with the lowest test error —
then you have used the test set to make a decision, and it is no longer untouched.
Its error will flatter the choice you tuned it to. You have leaked.

The standard fix is a *third* split: carve a **validation set** out of the training
data. Fit candidate models on the training set, compare them on the validation set,
choose the best, and only *then* — having committed — measure the chosen model once
on the still-pristine test set for an honest final number. The validation set is for
making decisions; the test set is for the one final, unbiased verdict. When data is
scarce, **cross-validation** reuses the data more cleverly by rotating which part
serves as validation — the subject of Chapter 11. The principle is constant: the
data you use to *choose* a model cannot also be the data you use to *judge* it.

## 9. Never touch the test set twice

Everything above reduces to one rule, worth burning into memory: **the test set is
sacred — look at it exactly once, at the very end.** Every peek costs you. Tune a
hyperparameter against it, and you have fit to it. Run twenty model variants and
report the best test score, and you have fit to it. Even glancing at it to "get a
feel for the data" leaks a little information into your choices. Each interaction
quietly inflates your estimate and erodes its honesty.

This is the discipline that separates trustworthy machine learning from the kind that
looks wonderful in a report and falls apart in production. It is not about the
algorithm at all; it is about not deceiving yourself. Hold the line, and your numbers
mean something.

## 10. Doing it yourself

Split, fit on train, evaluate on test, and watch the U emerge across complexity.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 40
x = np.linspace(-1, 1, n)
true = lambda x: np.sin(2.2 * x)
y = true(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.22, n)

# split: every 4th point is test, the rest train (shuffle first in real life)
is_test = (np.arange(n) % 4 == 0)
xtr, ytr = x[~is_test], y[~is_test]
xte, yte = x[is_test], y[is_test]

def mse(p, xs, ys):
    return np.mean((np.polyval(p, xs) - ys) ** 2)

print(f"{'degree':>6} {'train':>8} {'test':>8}")
for d in [1, 3, 6, 9, 12]:
    p = np.polyfit(xtr, ytr, d)          # fit on TRAIN only

`}
/>

Training error falls all the way down as the degree rises; test error bottoms out in
the middle and then climbs as the high-degree fits start memorising noise. The model
you would actually ship is the one at the bottom of that test column — never the one
with the lowest training error.

## 11. Problems

### Problem 1 — Why hold data back?

A conceptual question. (a) Why is a model's error on its own training data a poor
estimate of how it will perform in the future? (b) What exactly does the test set
estimate, and why must it be split off *before* fitting? (c) A colleague reports
"99% accuracy" but admits they chose their model by trying many and keeping the one
with the best score on the test set. Why is their 99% not trustworthy?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** A sufficiently flexible model can fit its training data arbitrarily well —
even perfectly — by memorising it, including the noise, which tells you nothing
about new data. Training error measures fit to examples already seen; it can be
driven to zero without any genuine learning, so it systematically *overestimates*
future performance.

**(b)** The test set estimates the model's error on *unseen* data drawn from the
same distribution — its expected real-world performance. It must be split off before
fitting (and never used during it) so the model has no chance to adapt to those
points; only then is its error on them an unbiased stand-in for error on the future.

**(c)** By selecting the model that scored best on the test set, they used the test
set to make a decision — they tuned to it. The reported 99% is the maximum over many
tries, which is optimistically biased: some of that score is luck that happened to
favour the chosen model on those particular test points. An honest estimate needs a
*separate* validation set for the choosing and a pristine test set for the single
final measurement.

</details>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def make(seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = np.linspace(-1, 1, 40)
    return x, np.sin(2.2 * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.22, 40)

print(f"{'deg':>3} {'train':>8} {'test':>8}")
best_d, best_te = 0, 1e9
for d in range(1, 11):
    tr = te = 0.0
    for s in range(12):
        x, y = make(s)
        m = (np.arange(40) % 4 == 0)
        p = np.polyfit(x[~m], y[~m], d)
        tr += np.mean((np.polyval(p, x[~m]) - y[~m]) ** 2)
        te += np.mean((np.polyval(p, x[m]) - y[m]) ** 2)
    tr, te = tr / 12, te / 12
    print(f"{d:>3} {tr:>8.3f} {te:>8.3f}")
    if te < best_te: best_te, best_d = te, d
print("best degree (lowest test error):", best_d)
`}
/>

Averaging over realisations smooths out the luck of any one split and reveals the U
cleanly: training error slides down forever, test error dips and then rises, and the
best degree sits at the bottom of the dip. That degree — chosen by *held-out* error —
is the one that balances bias against variance.

</details>

### Problem 4 — Bias or variance?

A conceptual question. For each symptom, say whether the model most likely suffers
from high bias or high variance, and what you would change: (a) train error and test
error are both high and about equal; (b) train error is near zero but test error is
large; (c) the fitted curve changes drastically when you refit on a slightly
different sample of the data.

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a) High bias** (underfitting). Both errors are high because the model is too
simple to capture the pattern even on the training data. Increase complexity — a
higher-degree model, more features, a more flexible family.

**(b) High variance** (overfitting). Near-zero training error with large test error
is the signature of memorising the training noise. Decrease complexity, add
regularisation (Chapter 9), or get more training data.

**(c) High variance.** Instability across different training samples is the
definition of variance — the model is keying on the particular noise of whichever
sample it saw. The same cures as (b): simplify, regularise, or gather more data so the
noise averages out.

</details>

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
x = np.linspace(-1, 1, 60)
y = np.sin(2.2 * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.25, 60)
idx = rng.permutation(60)

      f"{fit_and_err(d_leak, train, test):.3f}")

# HONEST: choose by validation error, report test error of that choice
d_honest = min(degrees, key=lambda d: fit_and_err(d, train, valid))
print(f"honest: chose degree {d_honest} by validation, reports test error "
      f"{fit_and_err(d_honest, train, test):.3f}")
`}
/>

The leaky procedure reports a flatteringly low number, because it searched over
degrees for the one that happened to do best on the test points — fitting to the test
set's particular noise. The honest procedure chooses on the validation set and only
*then* consults the test set, so its reported number is an unbiased estimate of
future error, even if it is a touch higher. The honest number is the one that will
hold up tomorrow.

</details>
