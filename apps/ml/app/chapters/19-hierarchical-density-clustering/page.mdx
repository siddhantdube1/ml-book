# Hierarchical and density-based clustering

*Beyond k-means: a tree of nested clusters that never asks for k, and a density
rule that traces any shape and shrugs off the outliers as noise.*

Hand k-means the two interleaving crescents from Chapter 7 — the moons every
classifier in this book has cut its teeth on — and ask it for two clusters. It
will think for a moment and then draw a straight line clean through the middle
of both, splitting each crescent in half and pairing the wrong halves together.
It is not a bug. k-means can only carve the plane into convex tiles around its
centres, and no two such tiles trace a pair of crescents. The shape was always
beyond it.

That failure is one of three. k-means makes you name the number of clusters
before it has looked at a single point; it can only find clusters that are round
and roughly equal in size; and it forces every point, including the lone outlier
halfway across the plane, into one cluster or another, because it has no concept
of a point that simply does not belong. For tidy, blobby data none of this
matters, and k-means is the right first tool. For everything else, we need
clustering that asks a different question.

This chapter builds two such methods, each dissolving k-means' limits from a
different direction. The first stops trying to produce one answer and instead
builds a whole tree of them — every point alone at the bottom, one cluster at
the top, and every clustering you might want in between — so you choose the
number of groups by where you cut, not before you start. The second throws out
the idea of a centre altogether and defines a cluster as a *dense region* of the
plane, which lets it trace the moons exactly, count its own clusters, and shrug
off the outliers as noise. By the end you will know when to reach past k-means,
and for which of the two.

## 1. Where k-means runs out

It is worth being precise about k-means' three limitations, because each one
motivates a piece of this chapter. First, **you must choose k in advance**.
Chapter 18 spent a whole section on the elbow and silhouette heuristics for
guessing it, precisely because the algorithm cannot discover the number of
clusters itself — it does what you tell it, even when you tell it wrong.

Second, **it only finds convex blobs**. k-means assigns each point to its
nearest centroid, which partitions the plane into straight-edged Voronoi tiles.
Any cluster that is not roughly a round, separable lump — a crescent, a ring, a
long filament — gets sliced apart, because no arrangement of centres can bend a
tile around it.

Third, **it has no notion of noise**. Every point is assigned to some cluster,
so a single far-flung outlier is dragged into the nearest group, dragging that
group's centroid toward it in turn. Real data is full of such stragglers, and
k-means cannot say "this one belongs to nobody".

Three limitations, two cures. We take them in turn.

## 2. Clustering as a hierarchy

The first idea refuses to commit. Instead of producing one partition of the
data, **hierarchical clustering** produces a nested family of all of them at
once, arranged as a tree. At the bottom of the tree every point sits alone in
its own cluster; at the top, a single cluster holds everything; and in between,
every intermediate grouping you might want.

The version we will use is **agglomerative** — bottom-up. Begin with $n$
clusters, one per point. Find the two clusters that are closest together and
merge them into one. Now there are $n - 1$. Repeat: find the closest pair, merge,
repeat, until a single cluster remains. The sequence of merges, and the
distances at which they happened, is the tree. Crucially, nowhere in this
process did we name a number of clusters — that choice comes later, and from
looking at the tree itself.

## 3. Agglomerative clustering, step by step

The algorithm is almost too simple to state: *merge the two closest clusters,
repeat.* What makes it powerful is the record it leaves behind — the
**dendrogram**, a diagram of every merge drawn at the height of the distance it
bridged. Watch it build.

<DendrogramBuilder />

Each step fuses the two nearest groups and adds a bracket to the dendrogram, its
height marking how far apart the two halves were when they joined. Early merges
are short — nearby points snapping together — and late merges are tall, bridging
the wide gaps between already-formed clusters. The finished tree contains every
clustering simultaneously, and you read one off by cutting horizontally: slide
the cut and the points recolour into however many clusters the cut produces. We
will make that cut precise in §5.

## 4. Linkage: what does "closest" mean?

There is one genuine choice hidden in "the two closest clusters". The distance
between two single points is obvious, but the distance between two *groups* is
not, and the definition you pick — the **linkage** — changes the clustering
completely.

<LinkageComparison />

Four linkages are standard. **Single** linkage takes the distance between the
two *nearest* members, one from each cluster — which means a cluster can grow by
reaching out through its closest point, and as the widget shows, a thin bridge
of points lets it *chain* two separate blobs into one. **Complete** linkage takes
the *farthest* pair, so a merge only happens when two clusters are compact and
close all over, producing tight balls and refusing the bridge. **Average**
linkage splits the difference, using the mean distance over all pairs. **Ward**
linkage merges the pair that least increases the total within-cluster variance —
the same sum-of-squares objective k-means minimises, which makes Ward the
blob-friendly default. Single linkage's chaining is its famous weakness and its
occasional superpower: it is exactly what lets single linkage trace a crescent.

## 5. Reading and cutting the dendrogram

The tree is the whole output; a *clustering* is a horizontal slice through it.
Choose a height, draw a line across the dendrogram, and every branch the line
cuts becomes one cluster — everything below that severed branch is grouped
together. Cut low, near the leaves, and you get many small clusters; cut high,
near the root, and you get a few large ones. Cutting at a height that yields
exactly $k$ groups is the same as stopping the agglomeration $k$ merges from the
end.

So where should you cut? The dendrogram answers visually. Look for the **tallest
vertical gaps** — the long stretches of height in which no merge happens. A big
gap means the clusters below it stayed stubbornly separate across a wide range of
distances before finally joining, which is the signature of a real division in
the data. Cutting across the tallest gap gives the most "natural" number of
clusters, and is the closest hierarchical clustering comes to choosing $k$ for
you.

## 6. A different question: density

The second method discards centres and trees alike and asks a question your eye
answers instantly when it picks out the moons: *where is the plane crowded?* A
cluster, in this view, is not a region near a centre but a **dense region** — a
patch where points are packed tightly together — and the boundaries between
clusters are the sparse gaps where the density drops away.

This reframing fixes all three of k-means' problems at a stroke. A dense region
can be any shape — a blob, a crescent, a ring — because density follows the
points wherever they go. The number of clusters is however many separate dense
regions there are, which the algorithm discovers rather than demands. And a point
in no dense region at all belongs to no cluster — it is simply *noise*. We need
only make "dense" precise.

## 7. DBSCAN: core, border, and noise

**DBSCAN** — Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise — makes
density mechanical with two parameters: a radius $\varepsilon$ and a count
`minPts`. Around each point, look within distance $\varepsilon$; if at least
`minPts` points fall inside (counting the point itself), the point sits in a
crowd and is called a **core** point. Clusters are then grown by chaining: start
at a core point, absorb everything within its $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood, and
for every absorbed point that is *also* core, absorb its neighbourhood too,
rippling outward through the dense region until the density runs out.

<DBSCANPlayback />

Three kinds of point fall out. A **core** point (filled) is dense — at least
`minPts` neighbours within $\varepsilon$, and the faint discs pile up to show
where the plane is crowded. A **border** point (ringed) is not itself dense but
lies within $\varepsilon$ of a core, clinging to a cluster's edge. A **noise**
point (grey ×) is neither — too isolated to reach any core — and is left
unclustered, the outlier k-means could never name. Notice there is no $k$: DBSCAN
chains together however many dense regions exist and counts them for you.

## 8. Choosing ε and minPts

The two knobs trade off against each other, and against the data's scale.
`minPts` sets how many neighbours make a neighbourhood "dense"; larger values
demand denser cores and label more points as noise, and a common rule of thumb is
to set it to about twice the number of dimensions. $\varepsilon$ is the sensitive
one — the radius that defines "close". Too small and even tight clusters fail the
density test and shatter into all-noise; too large and the gaps between separate
clusters fill in and everything fuses into one.

The standard way to choose $\varepsilon$ is the **k-distance plot**: for every
point, measure the distance to its `minPts`-th nearest neighbour, sort those
distances, and plot them. Most points (inside clusters) have small such
distances; the few in sparse regions have large ones, so the sorted curve stays
low and then bends sharply upward at an *elbow* — and the $\varepsilon$ at that
elbow is the radius that separates dense from sparse. And, exactly as in Chapter
17, because every step here is a distance, **standardise your features first** or
the largest-scaled one will define density on its own.

## 9. Arbitrary shapes, and the showdown

Time to settle the question the chapter opened with. Here are k-means,
single-linkage hierarchical, and DBSCAN, clustering the same data side by side.

<ShapeShowdown />

On round **blobs**, all three agree — when the clusters really are convex lumps,
k-means' assumptions hold and it is the fastest, simplest choice. Switch to the
**moons** or the **circles** and the picture splits apart: k-means draws its
straight partition straight through the shapes, confidently pairing the wrong
halves, while single-linkage hierarchical chains along each crescent and DBSCAN
grows along each dense band — both tracing the true forms, and DBSCAN quietly
marking the stray points as noise. This is the one-picture answer to "why not just
use k-means?": because the shape of your clusters is a fact about your data, and
only some algorithms can see it.

## 10. Choosing a clustering algorithm

There is no best clustering algorithm, only the one whose assumptions match your
data. A short guide, with the costs.

**k-means** (Chapter 18) is fast — $O(nki)$ for $n$ points, $k$ clusters, $i$
iterations — and scales to huge datasets. It needs $k$ up front, finds only
convex blobs, and assigns every point. Reach for it first on large, roughly-round
data.

**Hierarchical** clustering needs no $k$ in advance and hands back a whole
dendrogram to inspect, which is invaluable when you want to *understand* the
nested structure rather than just partition. But the naive algorithm is
$O(n^2 \log n)$ in time and $O(n^2)$ in memory — it holds the whole
pairwise-distance picture — so it is confined to smaller datasets, a few thousand
points at most.

**DBSCAN** finds clusters of any shape, discovers their number itself, and is the
only one of the three with a real notion of noise — superb for spatial data and
outlier detection. Its weaknesses are the flip side: it struggles when clusters
have very *different densities* (one $\varepsilon$ cannot be right for both), and
it is sensitive to that $\varepsilon$. Naive DBSCAN is $O(n^2)$, though spatial
indexes bring it close to $O(n \log n)$.

## 11. Implementing it yourself

DBSCAN from scratch — a region query, a queue that grows each cluster through its
core points, and the noise label for everyone left over — on the moons it was
built for.

def make_moons(n, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    half = n // 2
    cx, cy, r = 340, 200, 95
    pts = []
    for i in range(half):
        t = i / (half - 1) * np.pi
        pts.append([cx - 55 + r*np.cos(t) + rng.normal(0, 6),
                    cy + 25 - r*np.sin(t) + rng.normal(0, 6)])
    for i in range(n - half):
        t = i / (half - 1) * np.pi
        pts.append([cx + 55 - r*np.cos(t) + rng.normal(0, 6),
                    cy - 25 + r*np.sin(t) + rng.normal(0, 6)])
    return np.array(pts)

def dbscan(X, eps, min_pts):
    n = len(X)
    labels = np.full(n, -2)              # -2 unvisited, -1 noise, >=0 cluster id
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(2))
    region = lambda i: np.where(D[i] <= eps)[0]
    cid = -1
    for i in range(n):
        if labels[i] != -2:
            continue
        nb = region(i)
        if len(nb) < min_pts:            # not enough neighbours -> noise (for now)
            labels[i] = -1
            continue
        cid += 1                         # start a new cluster from this core point
        labels[i] = cid
        seeds = list(nb[nb != i])
        k = 0
        while k < len(seeds):            # grow the cluster through core points
            q = seeds[k]; k += 1
            if labels[q] == -1:
                labels[q] = cid          # a former noise point is a border point
            if labels[q] != -2:
                continue
            labels[q] = cid
            qnb = region(q)
            if len(qnb) >= min_pts:      # q is core -> chain onward
                seeds.extend(x for x in qnb if labels[x] in (-2, -1))
    return labels

X = make_moons(160, 5)
for eps in [10, 22, 40]:
    lab = dbscan(X, eps, 4)
    print(f"eps={eps:2d}: clusters={len(set(lab[lab >= 0]))}, noise={int((lab == -1).sum())}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
A = rng.normal([200, 200], 22, (30, 2))
B = rng.normal([480, 200], 22, (30, 2))
bridge = np.column_stack([np.linspace(200, 480, 10), np.full(10, 200.0)])

def agglomerate(linkage, target=2):

    while len(clusters) > target:
        best = (1e18, 0, 1)
        for a in range(len(clusters)):

the bridge one short hop at a time and fuses everything into one giant cluster

the same vulnerability from the same cause: make $\varepsilon$ large enough to
span the bridge and it, too, will chain the blobs into one — bridges are the
shared enemy of every density- or nearest-neighbour-based method.

---

The three clustering algorithms of these two chapters are not rivals so much as
different questions asked of the same scatter of points. k-means asks "which of k
centres is each point nearest?" — fast, simple, and blind to shape. Hierarchical
clustering asks "in what order do points and groups fuse as we relax our standard
for togetherness?" — and hands back a whole tree to read. DBSCAN asks "where is
the plane crowded, and where is it empty?" — and traces clusters of any shape
while naming the stragglers as noise. Knowing which question fits your data is
most of the skill of clustering.

All three, though, work in the space the data arrived in, taking its dimensions
as given — and that is the last assumption Part VI has left to question.
