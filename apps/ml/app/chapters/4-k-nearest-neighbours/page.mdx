# Your first model: k-nearest neighbours

*The simplest model in the book, and the only one with no equation: to predict
something about a new point, just look at the most similar points you have already
seen.*

You already use this algorithm, constantly, without naming it. Wondering whether you
will like a restaurant, you think of the most similar restaurants you have been to
and how those went. Guessing a stranger's profession, you compare them to people you
know who dress and talk the same way. Estimating a house's price, an agent looks at
what similar houses nearby recently sold for. The reasoning is the same every time:
*find the most similar past cases and let them vote.* That is **k-nearest
neighbours** — kNN — and it is the gentlest possible introduction to what a model is,
because it requires no formula, no fitting, and no calculus. It just remembers the
examples and consults them.

Despite its simplicity, kNN is a real, useful model, and it teaches in miniature
every big idea the rest of the book elaborates: a **decision boundary**, the
**bias–variance trade-off**, why **scaling** matters, and the **curse of
dimensionality** that haunts every method working with many features. Meet them here,
on a model you can fully understand in a sentence, and they will be old friends when
they return in more complicated company.

## 1. Prediction by analogy

Here is the whole idea, stated once. You have a collection of past examples, each
labelled with the answer you care about — emails marked spam or not, houses with
their sale prices, patients with their diagnoses. A new case arrives, unlabelled. To
predict its label, you find the example most similar to it (its *nearest neighbour*)
and copy that example's label. For robustness you look at not one but the $k$ nearest,
and take a majority vote (for classification) or an average (for regression).

There is no model to train in the usual sense — no parameters to fit, no loss to
minimise. The "model" *is* the dataset: kNN simply stores all the examples and, at
prediction time, searches them for the closest ones. This makes it almost trivially
easy to understand, and, as we will see, quietly expensive to use. But first, the
algorithm in action.

## 2. The algorithm: find the k nearest, take a vote

That is genuinely the entire algorithm, and it is easier shown than described.

<KNNVote />

To classify the diamond, kNN measures its distance to every labelled point, keeps
the $k$ closest, and takes their majority vote — the diamond is coloured by the
winner. Drag it around and watch the prediction flip the instant the local majority
flips. Raise $k$ and the decision is based on a wider circle of neighbours, so it
changes less abruptly. Notice what is *not* happening: there is no training step, no
equation being solved. The prediction is computed fresh, by looking, every single
time. This is "learning" in its most literal form — remember everything, and reason
by analogy when asked.

## 3. Distance, and why scale matters

"Nearest" needs a definition of distance, and the usual one is plain straight-line
(Euclidean) distance: $\sqrt{(x_1 - x_1')^2 + (x_2 - x_2')^2 + \dots}$, the dot
product's cousin from Chapter 3. But that formula hides a trap.

<KNNScaling />

If one feature is measured in thousands and another in fractions, the large-numbered
feature *dominates the distance* entirely — not because it matters more, but because
its numbers are bigger. The class here depends on both features equally, yet in raw
units kNN's boundary comes out nearly vertical, blind to the small-scale feature.
**Standardising** — rescaling every feature to a common spread, mean 0 and standard
deviation 1 — puts them on equal footing and the boundary corrects itself. Any model
built on distances is at the mercy of the units you happened to record, so for kNN
(and k-means, and much else) you almost always standardise first. Chapter 17 turns
this into a discipline; here, just remember: kNN measures distance, and distance has
units.

## 4. The decision boundary, and the role of k

Sweep $k$ across its whole range and the model's character transforms.

<KNNBoundary />

The shaded region is the **decision boundary** — the dividing line kNN draws between
the classes, the set of points where the vote is tied. With $k = 1$ the boundary is
wild and jagged: every training point, including the mislabelled and the unlucky, gets
its own little territory, so the model bends around every quirk of the data. This is
**high variance** — overfitting — the same failure as the wiggly curve of Chapter 2,
now in two dimensions. As $k$ grows, each prediction averages over more neighbours,
the territories merge, and the boundary smooths. Push $k$ too high and it
over-smooths, blurring across the genuine gap between the classes — **high bias**,
underfitting. The bias–variance trade-off of Chapter 2 is not an abstraction here; it
is a slider you can drag, and $k$ is the dial.

## 5. Choosing k

So what $k$ should you use? Exactly the answer Chapter 2 gave: not the one with the
lowest *training* error (that is always $k = 1$, which memorises perfectly and
generalises terribly), but the one with the lowest *held-out* error. Split off a
validation set, try a range of $k$ values, measure each on the held-out data, and
keep the best — typically a small odd number (odd, to avoid tied votes in two-class
problems). The validation error traces a U in $k$ just as it did in polynomial degree:
too small overfits, too large underfits, and the bottom is the $k$ that generalises.
The recipe is the workflow of Chapter 2, applied to the first model that needs it.

## 6. kNN for regression

kNN is not only a classifier. To predict a *number* rather than a category — a house
price, a temperature — change the vote to an **average**: find the $k$ nearest
neighbours and return the mean of their values. Want to know what a house should sell
for? Average the recent sale prices of the $k$ most similar houses. The same
remember-and-consult logic, with "majority vote" swapped for "average", turns kNN into
a regression model. Most classifiers in this book have a regression twin like this,
and kNN's is the most transparent of all.

## 7. A lazy learner

kNN has a peculiar cost structure that foreshadows why cleverer models exist.
Training is instantaneous — there is nothing to do but store the data. But
*prediction* is expensive: to classify one new point, kNN must compute its distance
to **every** stored example and find the closest, every single time. With a million
training points, every prediction is a million distance calculations. This is why kNN
is called a **lazy learner**: it defers all the work to prediction time, doing no
upfront summarising of the data into a compact model.

Contrast this with almost everything else in the book — linear regression, a decision
tree, a neural network — which are **eager** learners: they pay a one-time training
cost to distil the data into a small set of parameters, after which prediction is
cheap and the training data can be thrown away. kNN keeps the whole dataset forever
and recomputes from scratch each time. For small datasets this is fine and its
simplicity is a virtue; for large ones, the laziness becomes a liability, and the
eager models earn their keep.

## 8. The curse of dimensionality

kNN has one more weakness, and it is profound enough to have its own ominous name. The
whole method rests on "nearby points are similar" — but in **high dimensions, nothing
is nearby.** As the number of features grows, the volume of the space explodes,
points spread impossibly thin, and a strange thing happens: the distance to your
nearest neighbour and the distance to your farthest neighbour become almost the same.
"Nearest" stops meaning anything, because everything is roughly equidistant from
everything else.

This is the **curse of dimensionality**, and it cripples kNN (and every
distance-based method) when there are many features — dozens, hundreds, thousands.
With a handful of features kNN is excellent; with a thousand it is hopeless, because
the notion of a meaningful neighbour has evaporated. It is a major reason the field
develops models that do not lean so heavily on raw distance, and a major reason for
the dimensionality reduction of Chapter 20 — which fights the curse by squeezing many
features down to the few that matter.

## 9. When to reach for kNN

A fair summary. kNN is a fine choice when you have **few features**, **enough data**
to populate the space, **no need to explain** the prediction beyond "these similar
cases", and a dataset small enough that the per-prediction cost is acceptable. It is a
poor choice when features are many (the curse), when predictions must be fast on a
huge dataset (the laziness), or when the features are on wildly different scales and
unscaled (the distance trap). As a *baseline* it is invaluable: simple, assumption-free,
and a yardstick any fancier model should have to beat. Often it is the first thing to
try and the thing to improve upon — which is exactly why it opens the book's tour of
models.

## 10. Implementing it yourself

kNN from scratch is short, because there is so little to it: measure distances, take
the nearest, vote.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def make_blobs(n, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    y = np.arange(n) % 2
    cx = np.where(y == 0, -1.5, 1.5)
    X = np.stack([rng.normal(cx, 0.7), rng.normal(0, 0.7)], axis=1)
    return X, y

Xtr, ytr = make_blobs(120, 1)
Xte, yte = make_blobs(120, 2)

def knn_predict(Xtr, ytr, x, k):
    dists = np.sqrt(((Xtr - x) ** 2).sum(axis=1))   # distance to every example
    nearest = np.argsort(dists)[:k]                 # the k closest
    return int(ytr[nearest].mean() >= 0.5)          # majority vote

for k in [1, 5, 15]:
    acc = np.mean([knn_predict(Xtr, ytr, x, k) == y for x, y in zip(Xte, yte)])
    print(f"k = {k:>2}: test accuracy {acc:.3f}")
`}
/>

There is no training loop at all — `knn_predict` does everything at prediction time,
computing distances to all the stored points and voting. That is both the charm of
kNN and its cost: the simplest model imaginable, paid for one prediction at a time.

## 11. Problems

### Problem 1 — Understanding kNN

A conceptual question. (a) Why does kNN have no training step, and what is the "model"
it uses to predict? (b) Why does $k = 1$ achieve perfect accuracy on the training data
yet often generalise poorly? (c) Why must features usually be standardised before
running kNN? (d) Why does kNN break down when there are very many features?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** kNN does no upfront fitting — it simply stores the labelled examples. The
"model" *is* the stored dataset; prediction searches it for the nearest neighbours
and votes. There are no parameters to learn.

**(b)** Each training point is its own nearest neighbour (distance zero), so $k = 1$
labels every training point as itself — perfect training accuracy. But the resulting
boundary bends around every point, including noise and mislabels (high variance), so
it captures the quirks of the training sample rather than the underlying pattern and
does poorly on new data.

**(c)** kNN ranks points by distance, and an unstandardised feature with large numeric
range dominates the distance regardless of its true importance — so the model
effectively ignores the small-scale features. Standardising puts all features on a
common spread so each contributes fairly to "nearness".

**(d)** The curse of dimensionality: as features multiply, points spread thin and the
nearest and farthest neighbours become nearly equidistant, so "nearest" loses meaning.
kNN, which depends entirely on meaningful nearness, then fails.

</details>

In 2 dimensions the nearest point is far closer than the farthest — "nearby" is
meaningful. By 1000 dimensions the ratio is close to 1: the nearest and farthest points
are almost the same distance away, so "nearest neighbour" carries no information.
This is the curse, and it is why kNN — and any method that trusts raw distance — needs
few features, and why reducing dimensions (Chapter 20) matters so much.
