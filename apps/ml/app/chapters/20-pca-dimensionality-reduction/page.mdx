# PCA and dimensionality reduction

*Not grouping the points, but reshaping the space they live in — finding the few
directions that carry the data's real variation, and throwing the rest away.*

Open a dataset with a thousand columns — every pixel of a small image, every gene
in a tissue sample, every sensor on an engine — and you hit a wall immediately.
You cannot plot it; a scatter needs two axes and you have a thousand. You can
barely reason about distances in it, because in very high dimensions everything
is roughly equidistant from everything else. And the models that lean on
distance, from k-nearest neighbours to k-means, quietly fall apart. A thousand
numbers per example sounds like a thousand independent facts, and the trouble is
that it almost never is.

Because those columns are not independent. Pixels next to each other take nearly
the same value; genes switch on in concert; the engine's twenty sensors are all
reading off the same few underlying states. The data sprawls across a
thousand-dimensional box, but it does not fill it — it clusters near a thin,
low-dimensional sheet folded inside, and almost all of those thousand directions
are redundant or noise. The real information lives in a handful of directions, if
only you knew which.

Principal Component Analysis is how you find them. It rotates the data to a new
set of axes aligned not to your arbitrary original columns but to the data
itself — ordered so the first axis captures as much of the variation as any
single direction can, the second as much of what remains, and so on — and then
lets you keep the first few and discard the rest with almost no loss. It is the
automatic, learned version of the feature engineering you did by hand in Chapter
17, the standard way to compress data and to *see* it, and one of the most used
ideas in all of machine learning. By the end you will know exactly what those
axes are, where they come from, and how many of them you need.

## 1. Too many dimensions

The discomfort of high dimensions is worth naming precisely, because it is what
PCA relieves. Three problems compound. You **cannot visualise** more than three
dimensions, so any dataset wider than a few columns is invisible to you, and you
lose the single most powerful debugging tool there is — your eyes. Distances
**stop discriminating**: as dimensions grow, the nearest and farthest points
from any query drift toward the same distance, so "nearest neighbour" and "this
cluster" lose their meaning (the curse of dimensionality, met in earlier
chapters). And everything is **expensive** — storage, computation, and the amount
of data you need to fit a model all balloon with the number of features.

The escape is the fact that high-dimensional data is almost never *truly*
high-dimensional. Its features are correlated, so the points do not roam the
whole space; they lie close to a lower-dimensional surface within it. A dataset
of face photographs has tens of thousands of pixels, but faces vary along only a
few dozen meaningful directions — pose, lighting, expression — and the rest of
the pixel-space is empty. Find those few directions and you can replace a
thousand numbers with a few dozen, losing almost nothing. The question is how to
find them, and the answer begins with variance.

## 2. Variance is information

Here is the reframing the whole method turns on. Suppose you must throw away one
direction — collapse the data along one axis and keep the rest. Which axis is
safest to lose? The one along which the data **barely varies**. If every point
has nearly the same value in some direction, that coordinate carries almost no
information — knowing it tells you little about which point you are looking at, so
discarding it costs little. Conversely, a direction along which the points
spread *widely* is exactly where they differ from one another, which is to say
where the information is.

So "find the important directions" becomes a concrete, measurable goal: **find
the directions of maximum variance**. Reducing dimensionality is then keeping the
high-variance directions and dropping the low-variance ones. This is a modelling
assumption — that variance equals information — and it is usually a good one, but
not always; we will see in §10 where it misleads. For now, take it and run: the
most important direction in the data is the one along which it varies the most.

## 3. The first principal component

Make it concrete in two dimensions, where you can see it. Take a tilted,
elongated cloud of points and consider projecting them onto a line through the
centre — replacing each point with its shadow on that line, a single number.
Different lines preserve different amounts of the spread. Lay the line along the
cloud's long axis and the shadows spread out almost as much as the points did;
lay it across the short axis and they bunch together, their differences crushed.

<PrincipalAxes />

Rotate the line and watch the variance of the shadows swell and shrink. There is
one direction where it is largest — the long axis of the cloud — and that
direction is the **first principal component**, PC1: the single direction that
captures more of the data's variance than any other. It is the best possible
one-dimensional summary of the data, the line you would keep if you could keep
only one.

## 4. The rest of the components

One direction is a line, and usually we want to keep more than that. The second
principal component is found by the same maximise-variance rule, with one added
constraint: it must be **orthogonal** to the first. Among all directions
perpendicular to PC1, PC2 is the one of greatest remaining variance. PC3 is
perpendicular to both PC1 and PC2 and captures the most of what is still left,
and so on, each new component at right angles to all the previous ones and taking
the largest share of the variance that remains.

The result is a complete new set of axes — as many as the original dimensions —
that are **orthogonal** (a genuine rotation of the space, no shearing) and
**ordered** by how much variance each one captures. These are the principal
components, and they are aligned to the data's own shape rather than to the
arbitrary columns you happened to measure. Keeping the top few and dropping the
tail is dimensionality reduction; the orthogonality is what makes the kept
components non-redundant, and the ordering is what tells you which to keep.

## 5. Projection and reconstruction

Reducing a point to its top-$k$ components is **projection**: record only its
coordinates along PC1 through PC$k$ and forget the rest. A two-dimensional point
keeping one component becomes a single number — its position along the PC1 line.
To undo it, **reconstruct**: place that number back on the PC1 line in the
original space, which gives an approximation of where the point used to be.

<ProjectionReconstruction />

Keep one component and every point collapses onto the PC1 line; the orange stubs
are the **reconstruction error**, each point's perpendicular distance from the
line — exactly the part you threw away. Because PC1 is the maximum-variance
direction, those residuals are as small as any single line could possibly make
them. The variance you *kept* is $\lambda_1$ and the variance you *lost* is
$\lambda_2$, the variance along the discarded direction — and that is the
reconstruction error, no more and no less. Keep both components and the
reconstruction is exact. Dimensionality reduction is lossy compression, and the
loss is precisely the variance of the directions you dropped.

## 6. The mathematics: covariance and eigenvectors

Where do the components actually come from? Centre the data (subtract the mean of
each feature) and form the **covariance matrix**

$$C = \frac{1}{n} X^\top X,$$

a $d \times d$ symmetric matrix whose entry $C_{ij}$ is how features $i$ and $j$
vary together — the diagonal holds each feature's own variance, the off-diagonal
their correlations. The variance of the data projected onto a unit direction $u$
turns out to be exactly $u^\top C u$, so "find the direction of maximum variance"
is "find the $u$ that maximises $u^\top C u$" — and the answer, from linear
algebra, is the **top eigenvector** of $C$. The principal components *are* the
eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and each component's variance — its
$\lambda$ — is the corresponding **eigenvalue**. Because $C$ is symmetric, its
eigenvectors are automatically orthogonal, which is precisely the orthogonality
of §4, free of charge.

So all of PCA is one eigendecomposition of one symmetric matrix. In practice it
is computed not from $C$ but from the **singular value decomposition** of the
centred data matrix directly, which is more numerically stable and gives the same
components — the singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues. Either
way, the recipe is: centre, decompose, sort by eigenvalue, keep the top few.

## 7. How many components to keep?

The eigenvalues answer this too, because each one *is* the variance its component
captures. Divide a component's eigenvalue by the sum of all of them and you get
its **explained-variance ratio** — the fraction of the data's total spread that
component accounts for. List those ratios in descending order and plot them: the
**scree plot**.

<ScreePlot />

The bars fall off a cliff. A few components carry almost all the variance, and
the rest — the "scree", the rubble at the base of the cliff — carry almost none.
The black curve accumulates them, and the standard rule is to keep enough
components to reach a chosen threshold of cumulative variance, commonly 90% or
95%. Here three of eight components clear 90%, so you could store these points
with three numbers instead of eight and lose a tenth of the variance. The cliff's
edge is the data telling you its true, intrinsic dimensionality, buried under the
nominal one.

## 8. Standardise first

A trap that bites everyone once. PCA maximises variance, and variance has units —
so a feature measured in millimetres has a thousand times the variance of the
same feature in metres, and PCA, chasing variance, will point PC1 almost entirely
along whichever feature happens to carry the biggest numbers, regardless of
whether it carries the most *meaning*. A dataset mixing salaries in the tens of
thousands with ages in the tens would find PC1 pointing essentially along salary,
not because salary matters more but because its variance is numerically huge.

The fix is the one from Chapter 17: **standardise each feature** to mean 0 and
variance 1 before running PCA, unless the features are already in genuinely
comparable units (like pixels of one image). Running PCA on standardised data is
the same as running it on the **correlation** matrix instead of the covariance
matrix, and it is the safe default whenever your features are measured on
different scales. As ever with distance- and variance-based methods, scale is not
a detail.

## 9. Seeing high-dimensional data

Now the payoff that makes PCA indispensable, and the reason it closes the
unsupervised part of this book. Suppose your data has real structure — clusters,
say — but the directions that separate those clusters are spread across many
features, so no single pair of columns reveals them.

<Embedding2D />

Look at any two of the raw features and the three clusters smear into one
indistinct blob — the structure is real but invisible, because it does not align
with any original axis. Project the same points onto PC1 and PC2 and the clusters
spring apart into a clear picture, because PCA has rotated the space to look down
the directions of greatest variation, which here are exactly the directions that
separate the groups. This is dimensionality reduction as a *microscope*: a
hundred-dimensional dataset becomes a 2-D scatter you can actually read, and it
is almost always the first thing to do with unfamiliar high-dimensional data —
including, naturally, feeding the 2-D view straight into the clustering of
Chapters 18–19.

## 10. What PCA cannot do

PCA earns its ubiquity, but its assumptions are real and worth knowing. It is
**linear** — it can only rotate and project, so it captures structure that lies
along straight directions and misses structure that curves. Data wound onto a
spiral or a rolled-up sheet (the famous "Swiss roll") has a low intrinsic
dimension that PCA cannot find, because unrolling it is not a rotation; this is
why non-linear methods like **t-SNE** and **UMAP** exist, purpose-built for
visualising curved manifolds. PCA's components are also **blends of every
feature** — PC1 might be "0.3 × salary plus 0.5 × age minus 0.2 × height" — which
makes them powerful but often hard to interpret. And it is **unsupervised**: it
maximises variance, which is not always the direction that matters for a label —
the biggest source of variation in face photos might be lighting, useless if you
are trying to recognise identity.

*Complexity* is modest: forming and decomposing the covariance matrix is about
$O(\min(n d^2,\, d n^2))$, cheap for moderate dimensions, and randomised SVD
handles the very large cases by computing only the top few components directly.

## 11. Implementing it yourself

PCA from scratch — centre, covariance, eigendecompose, sort, project — and the
explained variance read straight off the eigenvalues.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

def pca_fit(X, standardise=False):
    mean = X.mean(0)
    std = X.std(0) if standardise else np.ones(X.shape[1])
    Z = (X - mean) / std                       # centre (and optionally scale)
    C = (Z.T @ Z) / len(X)                      # covariance matrix
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)             # symmetric eigendecomposition
    order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]             # sort by variance, descending
    return mean, std, vecs[:, order].T, vals[order]   # components are the rows

def project(X, mean, std, comps, k):
    return ((X - mean) / std) @ comps[:k].T   # n x k scores

# A tilted, elongated 2-D cloud — PC1 should land on its long axis.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
ang = 0.5
Rot = np.array([[np.cos(ang), -np.sin(ang)], [np.sin(ang), np.cos(ang)]])
X = np.column_stack([rng.normal(0, 2.5, 400), rng.normal(0, 0.7, 400)]) @ Rot.T

mean, std, comps, vals = pca_fit(X)
ratios = vals / vals.sum()
print("PC1 direction:", comps[0].round(3))
print("eigenvalues (variances):", vals.round(3))
print("explained-variance ratio:", ratios.round(3))
# the angle of PC1 (a line, so taken mod pi) matches the 0.5 tilt:
print("PC1 angle:", round(np.arctan2(comps[0,1], comps[0,0]) % np.pi, 3))
`}
/>

The eigenvector of the covariance matrix with the largest eigenvalue is PC1, and
its eigenvalue is the variance along it. The sign of a component is arbitrary — an
axis points both ways — so PC1 here may come out as either end of the long axis,
which is why we compare its angle modulo $\pi$. Everything else in PCA is
bookkeeping on top of this one decomposition.

## 12. Problems

### Problem 1 — Variance, axes, and limits

A conceptual question. (a) Why does "directions of maximum variance" capture "the
important directions" — what would it mean to discard a low-variance direction?
(b) Why must the principal components be orthogonal, and why are they ordered? (c)
Give one kind of structure PCA will fail to capture, and explain why
standardising the features can change the result completely.

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** A direction of low variance is one along which all the points have nearly
the same value, so that coordinate barely distinguishes one point from another —
it carries little information, and discarding it loses little. A direction of high
variance is where the points genuinely differ, so it is where the information
lives. Keeping high-variance directions therefore keeps the most information per
dimension retained.

**(b)** Orthogonality ensures the components are non-redundant: each captures
variation the others do not, so together they form a genuine rotation of the
space rather than overlapping, repeated directions. The ordering — by eigenvalue,
i.e. by variance captured — is what makes "keep the top k" meaningful: you keep
the directions that matter most and drop the least.

**(c)** PCA is linear, so it fails on data whose structure is curved — points
along a spiral or a rolled-up sheet have a low intrinsic dimension that no
rotation-and-projection can recover (you would have to *unroll* it). And because
PCA chases variance, which scales with units, standardising changes which feature
dominates: on un-standardised data a large-numbered feature can capture PC1 by
sheer magnitude; standardising puts every feature on equal footing and can point
the components somewhere entirely different.

</details>

---

Principal Component Analysis is the quiet workhorse of unsupervised learning: one
eigendecomposition, and a thousand tangled columns become a handful of ordered,
independent directions you can plot, store, and compute on. It compresses, it
denoises, it decorrelates, and above all it lets you *see* — turning structure
that was buried in a hundred dimensions into a picture on a page. Its limits are
equally clear: it is linear, it is unsupervised, and its axes are blends of
everything, so when the structure curves or the labels matter you reach for other
tools. But as a first look at almost any high-dimensional dataset, nothing beats
it.

It also closes the classical arc of this book. You can now take raw data, shape
its features, fit and tune supervised models across a wide repertoire, cluster
without labels, and reduce dimensions to see and compress. Every one of those
methods, though, drew its boundaries and axes from formulas you could write down
in closed form or a single greedy loop. The last part asks what happens when a
model is allowed to *learn its own features*, layer upon layer, with nothing but
gradient descent and a great deal of data.
