# Feature engineering

*The unglamorous, decisive craft of turning raw data into the columns a model
can actually use — where more problems are won and lost than in any choice of
algorithm.*

Two teams are handed the same dataset and the same week. The first team treats
it as a modelling problem: they reach for the most powerful algorithm they
know, tune its hyperparameters into the night, stack three models on top of one
another, and squeeze out every decimal the architecture will give. The second
team barely changes their model at all — plain logistic regression, untouched
since the first afternoon — and spends the week on the *columns* instead. They
split a timestamp into hour-of-day and day-of-week. They take the ratio of two
numbers that, alone, said nothing. They fold a sprawling category down to the
one number that mattered. When the scores come in, the second team has won, and
it is not close.

This is the open secret of applied machine learning: the model gets the glory,
but the features do the work. Given good features, a humble linear model is
hard to beat; given bad ones, no architecture on earth will save you.
Everything in this book so far has quietly assumed the features were already
there — that `x` arrived as a clean, sensible vector. This chapter is about
where that vector actually comes from, which is to say, about the most
consequential and least celebrated craft in the field.

By the end you will know why a feature measured in thousands can silently bully
one measured in fractions, how to make a straight-line model draw curves by
handing it the right columns, why encoding the hour of the day as a number from
0 to 23 is a small disaster, and the single most expensive mistake in all of
machine learning — the one that makes your scores look wonderful right up until
they meet the real world.

## 1. The unglamorous truth

It is worth stating plainly, because the field's incentives point the other
way. Papers are written about models; competitions are remembered for the
winning architecture; the glamorous word is "algorithm". But sit with
practitioners and you hear the opposite emphasis, over and over: the model is
rarely the bottleneck. Swap a gradient-boosted tree for a random forest and you
move a metric by a fraction of a percent; engineer one genuinely informative
feature and you move it by ten.

The reason is simple. Every algorithm in this book is a way of *combining* the
features it is given — drawing a boundary, averaging a neighbourhood, splitting
on a column. None of them can use information that is not present in the input,
and none can un-mangle information that the input presents badly. The features
define the space of questions the model is even able to ask. Choose them well
and the easiest model in the world has a clean problem to solve; choose them
badly and the hardest model is doomed before it starts.

So feature engineering is not preprocessing — a chore to finish before the real
work. It *is* the real work, for most problems most of the time. The rest of
this chapter is the toolkit.

## 2. A feature is a choice

Start by being precise about what a feature is. Raw data is whatever you were
given: a timestamp like `2026-03-14T23:47:00`, a postcode, a free-text review,
a price in yen sitting next to a count of clicks. A *feature* is a number (or a
small set of numbers) you derive from that raw material and place in a column of
the matrix `X` the model consumes. The timestamp is not a feature; the *hour of
day* you extract from it is. The review is not a feature; the *count of
exclamation marks*, or the *TF-IDF weight of the word "refund"*, is.

That gap — between raw data and feature matrix — is where all the decisions
live, and they are decisions, not formalities. Encode the postcode one way and
the model sees geography; encode it another and it sees noise. Every column is a
hypothesis about what matters, expressed in the only language the model
understands: numbers on comparable scales. The chapter's techniques are the
recurring patterns for getting from one to the other — and the first, and most
common, is the humblest.

## 3. Scale: putting features on equal footing

Most models have no idea what your numbers *mean*; they only see their sizes.
And anything that measures distance or follows a gradient is therefore at the
mercy of the units you happened to record. A k-nearest-neighbour classifier
(Chapter 4) finds "close" points by Euclidean distance; a feature that ranges
into the thousands contributes thousands to that distance while a feature in
[0, 1] contributes almost nothing — so the big-numbered feature wins, not
because it matters more, but because its numbers are bigger.

<FeatureScaling />

The class here depends on *both* features equally, but in raw units the second
one is invisible — the boundary comes out nearly vertical, and the query's
neighbours are chosen by the first feature alone. **Standardisation** — subtract
each feature's mean, divide by its standard deviation, so every column has mean
0 and standard deviation 1 — puts them on equal footing, and the model finally
hears both. (Min–max scaling to [0, 1] does the same job; standardisation is the
default.) The same affliction hits gradient descent (Chapter 6): mismatched
scales stretch the loss surface into a steep, narrow valley that the gradient
zig-zags down slowly. Scaling round it out. Note the one family that does *not*
care — tree-based models (Chapters 12–14) split one feature at a time and never
compare across columns, so they are immune to scale. For everything else,
standardise first.

## 4. Making linear models curve: basis expansion

Here is the most powerful move in the toolkit, and the one that most changes
what a simple model can do. A linear model draws straight boundaries — but only
*in the features you give it*. Hand it new columns built from the old ones and
"straight" in the new space becomes "curved" in the original.

<PolynomialFeatures />

On the raw features the logistic model is helpless — no straight line separates
the XOR checkerboard or the nested circles, and it sits near chance. But tick
the interaction column $x_1 x_2$ and the XOR pattern falls apart cleanly; tick
the squares $x_1^2, x_2^2$ and a circular boundary appears. The model never
changed — it is still drawing a straight boundary, now in a space with an extra
manufactured axis, and a plane in that space is a curve back here.

You have seen this idea before. This is precisely the **kernel trick** of
Chapter 15 — lift the data into a richer space where a line suffices — except
there the lift was implicit, hidden inside a kernel function, and here you build
the columns *by hand* and can read them off. **Polynomial features**,
**interaction terms** (products of features, which capture "this matters only
when that is also true"), and other **basis functions** are how a linear model
punches far above its weight. The cost, as ever, is that more columns mean more
ways to overfit — which is exactly why basis expansion and the regularisation of
Chapter 9 are natural partners.

## 5. Encoding categories

Models eat numbers, but the world is full of categories — colours, cities,
product types, postcodes. You cannot feed the string "Cairo" to logistic
regression; you must turn it into numbers, and *how* you do it matters more than
it first appears.

<CategoricalEncoding />

**One-hot encoding** turns a $k$-value category into $k$ binary columns, exactly
one of them hot — honest, because it implies no ordering, but it explodes the
column count when a category has thousands of levels (every postcode its own
column). **Ordinal encoding** maps the categories to integers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ —
compact, but it invents an order the model will believe: nothing says Quito is
"greater than" Lagos, yet the integer says so. **Target (mean) encoding**
replaces each category with the average label for that category — one column,
richly informative, and a favourite for high-cardinality features. But notice
what it does: it looks at the label `y` to build a feature. That is borrowing
from the very thing you are trying to predict, and done carelessly it is a
disaster — which brings us, in §11, to the cardinal sin. The right encoding
depends on the cardinality, whether order is real, and how carefully you can
guard the leak.

## 6. Cyclical and temporal features

Some features are periodic — the hour of the day, the day of the week, the month
of the year, the angle of a compass — and the naive encoding gets them
spectacularly wrong. Write the hour as the integer 0 to 23 and you have told the
model that 23:00 and 00:00 are 23 apart, the furthest two hours can possibly
be, when in fact they are a single hour apart. Any pattern that straddles
midnight is now invisible.

<CyclicalEncoding />

The fix is to respect the geometry: place the periodic quantity on a *circle*
with a sine/cosine pair, $\big(\sin\frac{2\pi t}{T}, \cos\frac{2\pi t}{T}\big)$,
so that the last value wraps smoothly back to the first. On the clock face 23:00
sits right next to 00:00, exactly as it should, and the late-night pattern
becomes learnable. The same respect for structure unlocks the rest of a
timestamp: a raw date is nearly useless, but explode it into day-of-week, month,
is-weekend, is-holiday, and days-since-signup, and a flat column becomes a rich
description of *when* — calendar structure the model could never have recovered
on its own.

## 7. Taming skew and outliers

Real-valued features are often badly behaved. Incomes, city populations, website
visits, transaction amounts — these are typically **right-skewed**, a great mass
of small values with a long tail of enormous ones, and that tail wreaks havoc:
it dominates distances, drags means around, and stretches gradients. The
standard remedy is a **log transform**, $x \mapsto \log(1 + x)$, which pulls the
tail in and turns a lopsided distribution into a roughly symmetric one — often
the single highest-leverage transform on tabular data. (Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson
are tunable generalisations; the latter handles zeros and negatives.) For
genuine outliers — a fat-fingered data-entry error, a sensor glitch — **clipping**
or **winsorising** (capping values at, say, the 1st and 99th percentiles) tames
the extreme without throwing the whole row away.

## 8. Missing data

Real datasets have holes, and most models cannot eat a blank. The crude response
— drop every row with any missing value — can quietly discard most of your data
and bias what survives, since the rows with gaps are rarely a random sample.
**Imputation** fills the holes instead: the mean or median of the column for a
quick fix, a model trained to predict the missing value for a careful one. But
there is a subtlety worth internalising: the *fact* that a value was missing is
often itself informative — an unanswered income question, a blank optional field
— so adding a **missingness indicator** column (1 if the value was missing, 0
otherwise) alongside the imputed value frequently earns its keep. Missingness is
data too.

## 9. Text and other raw signals

Free text is the raw signal feature engineering handles most often, and Chapter
16 already showed the core move: the **bag of words**, which turns a document
into a vector of word counts. The standard refinement is **TF-IDF** — term
frequency times inverse document frequency — which scales each word's count down
by how common the word is across all documents, so that ubiquitous words like
"the" are crushed while rare, discriminative words like "refund" are amplified.
The same instinct generalises beyond text: images become edges, colour
histograms, and texture statistics; audio becomes spectral coefficients. These
hand-built signal features are the historical workhorses — and, not
coincidentally, the very thing deep learning later learned to replace by
discovering its own features, the subject the final part of the book builds
towards.

## 10. Feature selection

More features are not automatically better. Irrelevant columns add noise,
redundant ones add nothing but cost, and both inflate variance and the risk of
overfitting — the curse of dimensionality, met again. So part of the craft is
*removing* features, and there are three families of method. **Filter** methods
rank each feature by a cheap, model-blind score — its correlation or mutual
information with the target — and keep the top ones. **Wrapper** methods search
over subsets of features, retraining the model to score each candidate set:
accurate, but expensive. **Embedded** methods let the model select as it fits —
most elegantly the L1 (lasso) regularisation of Chapter 9, which drives the
coefficients of useless features to exactly zero, performing selection and
fitting in one stroke. Often the most valuable feature is the one you delete.

## 11. The cardinal sin: leakage

Now the mistake that silently inflates every score and then collapses the moment
the model meets reality. **Data leakage** is letting information that will not be
available at prediction time sneak into your features — and its most common,
most insidious form is using the *test data*, or the *whole dataset*, to build
features that you then evaluate on that same data.

The target encoding of §5 is the classic trap. Replace each category with its
mean label computed over the *entire* dataset, and every row's feature has
quietly peeked at every other row's answer — including, when you cross-validate,
the rows you are about to test on. A column built this way can look strongly
predictive even when the category is pure noise, because it has memorised the
labels rather than learnt from them. The cross-validation of Chapter 11, which
was supposed to give you an honest estimate, now lies: it reports a wonderful
score that evaporates in production.

The rule that prevents it is absolute: **every transform is part of the model,
and must be fit on the training fold only.** Fit your scaler's mean and standard
deviation, your imputer's medians, your target encoder's category averages on
the training data, then *apply* those frozen parameters to the validation and
test data. Inside cross-validation, that means refitting every transform inside
each fold. Get this backwards — scale or encode using the full dataset before
splitting — and you have leaked, and you will not find out until it is expensive.
Problem 5 catches the leak in the act.

## 12. Implementing it yourself

The core transforms, in plain NumPy — and, crucially, fit on train and applied
to test, never the other way around.

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ── Standardisation: fit on train, apply to test ──
def fit_standardiser(X):
    return X.mean(0), X.std(0) + 1e-9
def apply_standardiser(stats, X):
    mu, sd = stats
    return (X - mu) / sd

Xtr = rng.normal([100, 0.5], [40, 0.2], (6, 2))
stats = fit_standardiser(Xtr)            # parameters learned from TRAIN only
print("standardised train:\\n", np.round(apply_standardiser(stats, Xtr), 2))

# ── Polynomial / interaction expansion ──
def expand(X):
    x1, x2 = X[:, 0], X[:, 1]
    return np.column_stack([x1, x2, x1**2, x2**2, x1 * x2])
print("\\nexpanded columns:", expand(Xtr).shape[1])

# ── One-hot encoding ──
def one_hot(values, k):
    return np.eye(k)[values]
print("\\none-hot of [0,2,1]:\\n", one_hot(np.array([0, 2, 1]), 3))

# ── Cyclical encoding of the hour ──
def cyclical(t, period):
    a = 2 * np.pi * t / period
    return np.column_stack([np.sin(a), np.cos(a)])
print("\\nhours 23 and 0 as (sin,cos):\\n", np.round(cyclical(np.array([23, 0]), 24), 3))

# ── Leakage-free target encoding (means from TRAIN only) ──
def fit_target_encoder(cats, y, k):
    base = y.mean()
    table = np.full(k, base)
    for c in range(k):
        m = cats == c
        if m.sum() > 0:
            table[c] = y[m].mean()
    return table
cats_tr = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2]); y_tr = np.array([1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0])
table = fit_target_encoder(cats_tr, y_tr, 3)   # fit on TRAIN
print("\\ntarget-encoding table (per category):", np.round(table, 2))
`}
/>

Every one of these returns *parameters* learned on the training data — a mean, a
table, nothing more — that you then apply to unseen data. That discipline is the
whole game: a transform is a tiny model, and a model fit on the test set is no
test at all.

## 13. Problems

### Problem 1 — Features over models, and the leak

A conceptual question. (a) Standardisation changes everything for k-nearest
neighbours and gradient descent but *nothing* for a decision tree. Why? (b)
Give a concrete example of data leakage and explain exactly how it makes a
cross-validation score optimistic. (c) Target encoding replaces a category with
its mean label. What does that buy you, and what is the risk?

<details>
  <summary>Show solution</summary>

**(a)** kNN ranks points by Euclidean distance and gradient descent steps along
a loss surface whose shape depends on the feature scales — both compare and
combine features *across columns*, so the relative sizes of those columns change
the answer. A decision tree only ever splits on *one feature at a time*,
thresholding it (`x_j < t`); rescaling that feature just rescales the threshold
and leaves every split — and the whole tree — identical. Trees are invariant to
any monotonic per-feature transform; distance- and gradient-based models are not.

**(b)** Example: you standardise the full dataset (computing each feature's mean
and standard deviation over *all* rows) and *then* split into train and test.
The test rows have now influenced the scaling parameters applied to the training
rows, and vice versa — information has crossed the split. More dramatically,
target-encode a category using the whole dataset and each feature value contains
the labels of the test rows. The model is, in effect, partly told the test
answers, so cross-validation reports a score it cannot reproduce on genuinely
unseen data.

**(c)** Target encoding turns a high-cardinality category (thousands of cities)
into a single, directly predictive column — compact and powerful. The risk is
leakage: because the column is built from `y`, computing it in-sample lets each
row peek at its own label (and, across a split, at the test labels), inflating
the score. The fix is to compute the category means out-of-fold — on data the
row was not part of.

</details>

<PyodideEditor
  initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
K, N = 50, 150
cats = rng.integers(0, K, N)
y    = rng.integers(0, 2, N)

def target_table(c, l, k):
    base = l.mean(); t = np.full(k, base)
    for j in range(k):
        m = c == j
        if m.sum() > 0: t[j] = l[m].mean()
    return t

# in-sample (LEAKED): table built from all data, including each row's own label
table = target_table(cats, y, K)
in_sample = ((table[cats] >= 0.5).astype(int) == y).mean()

oof = 0
for i in range(N):
    mask = np.arange(N) != i
    t = target_table(cats[mask], y[mask], K)
    oof += int((t[cats[i]] >= 0.5) == y[i])
oof /= N

print(f"in-sample accuracy:   {in_sample:.3f}   <- looks predictive (LEAKED)")
print(f"out-of-fold accuracy: {oof:.3f}   <- the truth (~chance)")

The category is random — it has no relationship with the label whatsoever — yet
in-sample target encoding makes it look distinctly predictive, because with only
a few rows per category each encoded value is dominated by the row's own label.
Compute the same feature out-of-fold and the illusion vanishes back to chance.
This is exactly how leakage flatters a model in cross-validation and then betrays
it in production, and exactly why every transform must be fit inside the fold.

Feature engineering is where domain knowledge enters machine learning. The
algorithms in this book are general — they know nothing about spam, or houses,
or heartbeats — and it is the features that carry what you understand about the
problem into a form the algorithm can use. That is why it rewards curiosity
about the data over cleverness about the model, and why it remains, even now,
the part of the work that most separates a good practitioner from a great one.

With this chapter the supervised half of the book is complete: you can take raw
data, shape it into features, fit a model from a wide repertoire, tune it
honestly, and read its errors. What you have never done is work *without
labels*.

*Next: Chapter 18 — k-means clustering.* Part VI leaves labels behind. When no
one tells you the answers, can a model still find the structure hiding in the
data — the groups, the patterns, the hidden axes? Unsupervised learning begins.
